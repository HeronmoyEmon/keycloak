Case Study: Northwind Apps — Multi-Tenant SaaS IAM Design

A full architecture write-up for Northwind Apps, one of this course's three running case-study organizations (chapter 2). This page assembles, in one place, every identity decision Northwind's realm already embodies across chapters 2-45 — the business context, the realm/client architecture, the tenancy-model decision log (including what was considered and rejected), the enterprise-SSO design, the authorization model, a security hardening checklist, and what an IAM specialist should take away from it. Read this the way you'd read a real design document handed to you before a review meeting.

1. Business and compliance context

Northwind Apps sells a multi-tenant B2B SaaS dashboard product to other businesses. Its customers range from small companies with a handful of users on Northwind-local accounts to large enterprise customers (like Acme Corp) with their own corporate identity provider and hundreds of employees who need access. Northwind's own internal workforce (engineers, support staff, platform engineers) is a second, entirely separate identity population, managed in its own internal Active Directory (chapter 4) and never mixed with the customer-facing identity store. Because Northwind sells into the EU, GDPR obligations apply to every one of those customer identities (chapter 11) — a constraint that shaped the data residency and processor-agreement questions this write-up doesn't re-derive, but assumes.

In market terms (chapter 12), Northwind is squarely a CIAM buyer: thousands of end-customer logins across many tenant companies, a bursty, consumer-scale traffic pattern, and growing demand from its larger customers for enterprise SSO into its own product.

Key idea

Every concrete configuration below is the real, cumulative state of the northwind-apps Keycloak realm this course has built against, lab by lab, since chapter 20 — not a hypothetical redesign. This write-up's job is naming the decisions that state represents and defending them.

3. The tenancy model decision

This is the design's central, defensible decision, and the one most likely to come up in an interview: should each customer tenant get its own Keycloak realm, or should all tenants share one realm distinguished by a claim?

Shared realm + tenant_id claim (chosen)Realm-per-tenant (considered, rejected)
One admin context to manage, regardless of tenant countOne admin context per tenant — thousands at Northwind's scale
A realm-wide policy change (password policy, auth flow) applies onceThe same change has to be scripted and re-applied per realm
Tenant isolation enforced at authorization time (resource-scope checks)Tenant isolation enforced structurally by the platform itself
Scales linearly in storage/complexity with user count, not realm countScales linearly in operational cost with tenant count
A missed resource-scope check is a real cross-tenant bug class to guard againstA realm boundary can't be crossed by a role-check bug the way a shared realm's can

The deciding factor was scale, not isolation quality in the abstract: realm-per-tenant gives a cleaner isolation boundary, but Northwind has thousands of tenants today and growing, and an isolation model whose management cost grows linearly with tenant count stops being affordable long before the tenant count gets large. The trade Northwind accepted explicitly: tenant isolation becomes a discipline enforced in application and authorization code (every role check, every data query, must carry and verify tenant_id) rather than a guarantee the platform enforces for free. This is the same resource-scope lesson chapter 6 teaches generally, applied as a load-bearing design decision rather than a side note.

Pitfall

A role check that verifies "does this user hold tenant-admin" without also verifying "...scoped to this tenant_id" is the single most consequential bug class this design has to defend against. Every engineer touching an authorization check in this system needs to internalize that the role name alone is never sufficient.

4. Enterprise SSO: brokering, not federation, not shared passwords

Enterprise customers like Acme Corp want their employees logging into Northwind with their own corporate identity. Northwind evaluated three options:

  • Shared/synced passwords — rejected outright: it means Northwind storing or touching a credential that properly belongs to Acme's own IT department, with no way for Acme to revoke access to Northwind specifically without touching their whole directory.
  • Federation — rejected: federation means Northwind directly querying or binding against Acme's directory (the shape Riverside Health uses for its own clinician workforce against its hospital's AD, chapter 32). Northwind has no operational reason to run that kind of direct directory integration for every enterprise customer it signs.
  • Identity brokering — chosen: Northwind configures a trust relationship with Acme's own IdP (northwind-corporate-idp in this course's labs) and lets that IdP perform the actual login. Northwind never sees or stores Ben Carter's real Acme password; it only ever sees an assertion it has agreed to trust.

Before this was built (chapter 27's manual bulk-onboarding era), each new Acme hire meant Northwind's ops team manually creating an account by hand — a real, necessary stopgap, but one that doesn't scale past a customer's first handful of users and has to be repeated on every hire or departure. After brokering, a brand-new Acme employee gets a working northwind-apps identity automatically on first login (JIT provisioning), and Acme's own IT department remains the single source of truth for who currently counts as an Acme employee — deprovisioning on Acme's side is what actually removes their Northwind access, with zero Northwind-side manual step required.

Northwind's smaller, self-serve customers get a second brokered source — "Login with Google" — and its smallest customers, with no IdP of their own and no interest in one, keep ordinary Northwind-local accounts. All three identity sources land in the same realm, each tagged with the same tenant_id discipline.

5. Authorization model: roles plus resource-owner delegation

Per-tenant RBAC (chapter 6) is the primary model: "what's this user's job within their tenant" is a stable, role-shaped question, as long as every assignment carries its tenant scope. But Northwind's document-sharing feature needed an answer RBAC structurally can't give — "did the owner of this one specific document choose, at some point, to share it with this one other specific user" — a decision made at runtime by the resource's owner, not encodable as a role an admin assigns ahead of time. Chapter 37's lab built this with Keycloak's Authorization Services (UMA 2.0): a resource server registers protected resources and scopes, the resource owner grants permission tickets, and every access request is checked against both the role model and the permission-ticket model before it's allowed through.

Framed against chapter 38's broader comparison: Northwind's RBAC layer is a textbook role model, and its UMA delegation layer behaves closer to ReBAC in spirit ("does a specific relationship — ownership plus an explicit share — exist between this user and this resource") even though it's implemented via UMA's permission-ticket mechanism rather than a Zanzibar-style relationship graph. Keycloak doesn't force a single authorization paradigm; Northwind uses the two paradigms that actually answer its two different questions.

6. Security hardening checklist

Chapter 9's Acme-customer XSS incident is the organizing case for this section: an attacker exploited a bug in Acme's own browser-based integration code, not anything Northwind wrote, and used it to read a stolen Northwind-issued bearer token. Northwind's own code was never at fault — but Northwind's blast radius was entirely a function of choices made in advance. The checklist below is those choices, made explicit:

  • Exact-match redirect_uri registration (chapter 13) — no wildcard or domain-level match for any registered client, closing off a whole class of authorization-code redirection attack regardless of what else lives on a customer's domain.
  • Short, deliberately bounded access-token lifetimes and scopes (chapters 8, 21) — a stolen token is dangerous only for as long as it's valid and only for what it's scoped to; both are choices Northwind controls directly, independent of any customer-side bug.
  • An absolute SSO Session Max ceiling, even while SSO Session Idle was lengthened (chapter 21) — two timers moving in different directions on purpose: idle timeout loosened for a B2B dashboard's genuinely long, low-stakes work sessions; the absolute ceiling tightened because it's the one number that bounds worst-case exposure no matter how "idle" looks from the session's perspective.
  • Admin REST API audience/scope check plus client policies (chapter 45) — defense for the realm's own administrative surface, independent of the customer-facing incident above but part of the same "assume something will go wrong, bound the damage" posture.
  • Event logging for every authentication and authorization decision (chapter 28) — without it, a postmortem for an incident like Acme's has no way to reconstruct what the stolen token was actually used for, or when access should have been (and was) cut off.

7. Lessons for an IAM specialist

  1. "Use RBAC" and "use Keycloak" are not designs — the design is the specific resource-scope discipline, tenancy model, and set of rejected alternatives that make a role model actually hold up at real scale.
  2. An isolation decision (realm-per-tenant vs. shared realm) is an operational-cost decision as much as a security decision; evaluate it against the actual number of tenants, now and at expected scale, not against isolation quality in the abstract.
  3. Brokering and federation are not interchangeable, and picking the wrong one for a given relationship (who owns the directory, who performs the actual authentication) produces an architecture that doesn't match how responsibility is actually distributed between the two organizations.
  4. A security incident caused by a partner's or customer's own code is not a reason to skip asking what your own system's configuration did to the blast radius — token lifetime and scope are usually the two most consequential answers.
  5. Two authorization models can coexist deliberately in one system because they answer two genuinely different questions; the specialist's job is naming, for any given access decision, which question is actually being asked.
