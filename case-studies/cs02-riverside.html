Case Study: Riverside Health

A reference-grade write-up of the IAM design chapter 47 built up piece by piece across this course. Read this as you would a design document handed to a hiring panel, a compliance auditor, or a new teammate joining the identity team — full configuration detail, every alternative considered and why it was rejected, and the HIPAA-control-to-mechanism mapping in full. For the reasoning walked through in the order it was taught, read chapter 47 first.

Key idea

Riverside Health is a regional hospital network running two identity populations under one regulator: a clinician workforce that needs fast, low-friction access from shared clinical workstations, and a patient portal population that needs ordinary consumer-grade self-service. Both sit inside the HIPAA Security and Privacy Rules. The entire design below answers one question for each population: what is the minimum authentication and authorization strength that still satisfies 45 CFR §164.312 and §164.502(b), without making either population's daily experience worse than it has to be?

1. Business and regulatory context

Riverside's identity population splits into two groups with almost nothing in common operationally:

  • Clinician workforce — doctors, nurses, and administrative staff, authenticating mostly from shared clinical workstations, where every second of login friction has a real clinical cost.
  • Patients — self-registering portal users who view lab results, message their care team, and pay bills, on their own devices, at their own pace.

Two HIPAA provisions drive nearly every decision that follows:

CitationRequirementWhat it demands of IAM
45 CFR §164.312(b) — Security Rule, Technical SafeguardsAudit controls Hardware/software/procedural mechanisms that record and examine activity on systems holding ePHI — concretely, admin-event logging with enough detail to answer "who, what, when."
45 CFR §164.312(a) — Security Rule, Technical SafeguardsAccess control Unique user identification, and access limited to what a role/relationship actually requires — concretely, per-realm authentication strength plus attribute-based authorization.
45 CFR §164.502(b), §164.514(d) — Privacy RuleMinimum necessary Limit PHI access requests to the minimum needed for the purpose at hand — concretely, the department-plus-shift ABAC rule and the care-team concept it approximates.

3. Clinician federation: a decision log

Option consideredOutcome
Migrate ~10,000 clinician accounts into Keycloak's native user store Rejected. Creates a second copy of identity data that drifts from AD's shift schedules and department assignments; HR would need to manage joiners/leavers in two places.
Federate against AD in READ_ONLY edit mode Chosen. Keycloak authenticates against the authoritative source without ever becoming a second place a credential can originate a change from.
Federate against AD in WRITABLE edit mode, enabling Keycloak-driven self-service password reset Rejected. Convenient for the clinician, but hands Keycloak's bind credential real write authority over a system the hospital's directory-operations team separately change-controls and audits. Password resets instead route through the hospital's existing AD self-service tooling.

4. Authorization model: minimum necessary as policy, not just principle

The governing requirement — a clinician may view a patient record only if their department matches the patient's assigned department, and only during their scheduled shift — decomposes into two Keycloak Authorization Services policies combined with the Unanimous decision strategy (both must agree; see the pitfall below for the composition bug this guards against):

  • Department policy — a group/attribute-based policy: clinician.department == record.department.
  • Shift-time policy — a time-based policy: current time falls inside the clinician's configured shift window.

This is the practical, natively-supported approximation of a true "currently on this patient's care team" relationship, which would be a ReBAC relationship edge rather than an attribute — Keycloak's Authorization Services has no relationship-tuple concept, so a department+shift rule is the ceiling of what's natively expressible without reaching for an external ReBAC engine (not needed for this version of the system).

Pitfall — decision strategy, not policy logic

If a clinician can see a record outside their department or outside their shift, check the permission's decision strategy first. Each individual policy can be written correctly and still produce the wrong combined result if the permission uses Affirmative (either policy passing is enough) instead of Unanimous — a composition bug invisible from reading either policy in isolation.

6. Break-glass access: the deliberate exception

Minimum necessary cannot be absolute in an emergency: an attending physician covering an unplanned case may need a record before any care-team relationship or department assignment is in place. Rather than loosen the standing ABAC rule — which would weaken minimum necessary for every ordinary request to accommodate a rare one — Riverside's design adds a separate, explicit exception path.

A clinician who fails the standing policy is offered a self-attested "emergency access" required action. Accepting it grants time-boxed access (one shift) and triggers a dedicated, high-visibility admin event on every use — flagged for same-week compliance review, distinct from the ordinary event stream. The trade accepted here is explicit: break-glass can occasionally be used when strictly unnecessary, in exchange for never blocking access during a genuine emergency. The audit, not the gate, is what makes this acceptable — and repeated break-glass use by the same person for the same department is itself a signal to review that department's underlying attribute data, not a disciplinary trigger on its own.

7. Audit controls: implementing 45 CFR §164.312(b)

Admin events with adminEventsDetailsEnabled=true produce a timestamped CREATE/DELETE pair for any role-mapping elevation (such as a shift-based access grant and its later revocation), each attributable to a specific admin user id via authDetails.userId, with operationType/resourceType/ resourcePath naming what changed and representation showing the exact before/after value. That satisfies "who had access, when, and who approved it" directly.

Pitfall — the audit trail can itself over-retain PHI

representation's full before/after value can capture more of a changed record's contents than a minimum-necessary review wants sitting in a separate event store indefinitely. The fix is retention and field-level redaction policy on the event store — not disabling adminEventsDetailsEnabled, which would remove the audit trail the Security Rule requires in the first place.

8. Security hardening checklist

  • Clinician federation stays READ_ONLY; no Keycloak-originated writes ever reach the hospital AD.
  • Session timeouts differ by realm and are reviewed against actual shared-workstation turnover patterns, not copied from a generic default.
  • Every authorization permission touching patient records uses the Unanimous decision strategy where more than one policy is involved; this is spot-checked after any policy change, not just at initial setup.
  • Admin events are enabled on both realms; the patient realm's event retention and the clinician realm's event retention are reviewed separately, since their respective minimum-necessary exposure differs.
  • Break-glass usage is reviewed weekly, specifically looking for repeated use against the same department (a data-quality signal) rather than only looking for individually suspicious single events.
  • Step-up required actions are tested after every realm upgrade — a regression here silently downgrades a sensitive action back to baseline authentication strength.

9. Lessons for an IAM specialist

  • A regulatory requirement like "minimum necessary" rarely maps to one Keycloak feature — it's usually a composition of several (ABAC policies, a decision strategy, a required action, an audit setting), and the job is choosing the right combination and writing down why.
  • Realm separation is a legitimate answer to "these two populations need different security postures," not an over-engineered one — the cost of maintenance is real but smaller than the cost of one group silently inheriting the wrong policy.
  • Every hard rule in a clinical or safety-critical system needs a deliberate, audited exception path; refusing to design one just pushes the exception into an unofficial workaround nobody logs.
  • The audit mechanism that satisfies a compliance requirement can itself become a new compliance surface (PHI retained in an event log) — auditing the auditor's own output is part of the job.
  • Federation edit mode (READ_ONLY vs. WRITABLE) is a security decision, not a convenience setting — it decides how many systems can originate a credential change for the same identity.
