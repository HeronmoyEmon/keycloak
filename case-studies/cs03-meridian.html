Case Study: Meridian Trust

A retail bank, PCI-DSS v4.0.1 regulated, running one Keycloak realm that serves two very different identity populations — bank employees and retail customers — under heavy fraud pressure. This is the full architecture write-up: every decision this course's labs made for Meridian (chapters 2-45), pulled into one document with a decision log and an audit-ready checklist. It's written to be read on its own, and to double as interview material for "design IAM for a regulated bank" questions.

Key idea

Nothing here is invented for this write-up. Every realm, client, role, and policy decision below was built and verified against a real Keycloak 26.8.2 + PostgreSQL 17 instance in its originating chapter's lab (see the "Chapter" column throughout) — this document organizes those decisions into one coherent system rather than introducing new ones.

1. Business and compliance context

Meridian Trust is a retail bank with two identity populations: bank employees — tellers, loan officers, back-office staff, and wire-transfer approval staff, each needing a different authorization profile — and retail customers who bank online and through a mobile app (chapter 2). Because Meridian's online banking platform touches payment card data, it carries a defined Cardholder Data Environment (CDE): the systems, people, and processes that store, process, or transmit cardholder data, plus anything connected to them (chapter 11). That puts Meridian squarely inside PCI-DSS v4.0.1's scope, with three requirements mapping directly onto this course's IAM vocabulary:

PCI-DSS is mandatory because Meridian processes card payments — but it is not Meridian's only obligation. As a bank, Meridian almost certainly needs a SOC 2 report too (enterprise banking partners and fintech integrators won't sign a contract without one), and if it has any EU retail customers or EU-based staff, GDPR applies as well (chapter 11). Treating Meridian as "just a PCI-DSS company" would miss two more compliance programs that the exact same IAM controls — access reviews, MFA, logging — are simultaneously satisfying. This write-up treats PCI-DSS as the primary driver because it's the most IAM-prescriptive of the three, but the controls below are written to hold up under all three.

2. Realm and client architecture

Meridian runs one realm, meridian-trust, created in chapter 20's lab and extended through chapters 23, 29, 30, 36, and 45. The alternative — a separate meridian-employees realm and meridian-retail realm — was a real option: a realm is Keycloak's unit of complete isolation (distinct users, roles, clients, themes, and signing keys, chapter 21). It lost to the single-realm design for a specific reason: employees and retail customers at Meridian never need authentication-level isolation from each other the way two unrelated multi-tenant customers would — they need authorization-level isolation, which realm roles, groups, and per-client scopes already provide inside one realm, without duplicating themes, brute-force policy, and key-rotation operations across two realms that would otherwise need to stay in lockstep.

4. Authorization model: three layers, not one

Meridian's authorization model stacks three distinct mechanisms, each answering a question the one below it structurally cannot (chapters 6, 10, 23, 38):

  1. RBAC — can this person act at all? The transfer-creator and transfer-approver realm roles, assigned via groups, modeled as mutually exclusive so no single provisioning action can create the toxic combination chapter 10 named: one identity able to both originate and approve the same wire transfer, erasing the independent check Requirement 7 exists to guarantee.
  2. ABAC — within what limit? A transfer-approver's actual dollar ceiling is computed from attributes — the employee's seniority tier and the destination account's risk tier — not from the role fact alone. Two employees holding the identical role can have different ceilings.
  3. ReBAC — on whose accounts, specifically? "An employee may approve a transaction only if they are the manager of the branch that owns the account" is a genuine two-hop relationship (manager_of(branch) and owns(branch, account)) that neither a role nor an attribute field can express cleanly. Chapter 38 evaluated and rejected the native-Keycloak approximations (a group per branch; a delimited attribute string) as approaches that degrade further with every added branch or every manager rotation. Meridian's documented target state, past a certain branch count, is an external relationship store — OpenFGA or an equivalent Zanzibar-derived engine — holding the relationship tuples, queried by a custom Keycloak Policy provider at approval time. Keycloak keeps authenticating the employee and issuing their token; it stops being the system of record for which branch they manage, because that was never an authentication-system fact.
Pitfall

A toxic combination fixed at grant time isn't fixed forever. Role assignments drift — a loan officer temporarily covers wire-transfer duty during a staffing gap — and the toxic pairing can form silently later, with no single provisioning action looking wrong in isolation. This is why Meridian's access-review cadence (chapter 10's IGA territory) sits on top of Keycloak rather than being expected from it: Keycloak has no built-in SoD-conflict detection, by design — that's IGA product territory, not IAM product territory (chapter 12).

5. Authentication: baseline MFA, and risk-based step-up

Meridian's realm-wide authentication baseline (chapter 30) is password plus a one-time code — the course's own AAA worked example — backed by a password policy that requires length, mixed case, digits, and special characters, plus notContainsUsername specifically, and a brute-force detection policy tuned stricter than Keycloak's own defaults on failureFactor and waitIncrementSeconds — but deliberately never escalating to permanent lockout, because a retail bank's customer base is itself a realistic denial-of-service target: an attacker who can lock out legitimate customers by deliberately failing their logins has simply found a different attack.

On top of that baseline, Meridian applies risk-based step-up authentication (chapter 5) to one specific action: initiating a wire transfer. Routine banking — checking a balance, reviewing a statement — uses the normal factor combination. A wire transfer triggers a risk evaluation across contextual signals (a new payee, an unusual amount, a device never seen on this account before); only when that combination crosses a threshold does the flow interrupt to demand an additional factor — a push approval or OTP — before funds move. A $20 transfer to a payee paid monthly for two years from the usual phone sees no extra prompt; a first-time $20,000 transfer from an unrecognized browser does. Mechanically this is a conditional execution inside a Keycloak authentication flow (chapter 25), gated by a Condition - User Role/Attribute execution and triggered via the requesting client's acr_values/claims request, or by the resource server demanding a fresher, stronger token before authorizing the transfer itself.

This design was validated against a real incident (chapter 9): a spike in failed logins traced not to any weakness in Meridian's own password hashing or storage, but to an unrelated retail-site breach whose leaked passwords a measurable fraction of Meridian's customers had reused on their banking login. The control that actually limited the damage was step-up authentication on wire transfers, not a stronger baseline password policy — credential-stuffed logins that succeeded still couldn't move money without clearing the step-up challenge.

6. Session and token lifecycle

Chapter 29 built Meridian's incident-response runbook for a stolen device: a teller's laptop is reported stolen mid-shift, still logged into Meridian's internal banking console. The lesson, proven against a live instance rather than narrated: a 204 response from a logout or revocation call only confirms the server accepted the request, not that the session is actually gone. The verified runbook is two steps — enumerate the user's active sessions, then issue a targeted DELETE /admin/realms/meridian-trust/sessions/{sessionId} for the specific stolen device's session (not a blanket POST users/{id}/logout, which would also log the teller out of their own phone) — followed by re-listing sessions to confirm none remain for that device. This targeted-revocation-then-reverify pattern is exactly the evidence a PCI-DSS Requirement 10 reviewer would ask Meridian to demonstrate.

7. Service-to-service: the fraud-detection pipeline

Meridian runs a nightly fraud-scoring pipeline — unattended, no browser session, no human operator — that pulls account and user metadata out of Keycloak to feed a risk model (chapter 36). Before that chapter's fix, its client held the manage-users client role from realm-management "just in case a later feature needed write access": textbook over-provisioning (chapter 9), and precisely the kind of finding a PCI-DSS least-privilege review flags on sight. The corrected design: a fraud-detection-service confidential client, authenticated via the client_credentials grant, whose service account holds only view-users. Verified directly against a live instance: the pipeline still completes its actual read-only job, and any write attempt now fails immediately and loudly — a finding closed before an assessor ever has to raise it.

8. Hardening and audit posture

Chapter 45 turned Meridian's accumulated controls into audit evidence against PCI-DSS Requirements 7, 8, and 10 specifically, plus two further items worth calling out on their own:

  • Admin console isolation. Keycloak's /admin path is kept off the same public-facing reverse-proxy path that serves customer login traffic, with the 26.8.2 audience check as a second layer — because a retail bank's Keycloak realm sitting behind the same public proxy that serves real customer traffic makes SSRF-class issues specifically dangerous: a successful SSRF against that proxy could otherwise become a path into Meridian's own internal network, not just an information leak.
  • Upgrade strategy. PCI-DSS Requirement 6's patching expectation is the direct argument for keeping Meridian's Keycloak version current rather than deferring upgrades — several real CVEs in the 26.8.x line (chapter 41-45's security findings) are exactly the kind of unpatched-software finding Requirement 6 is written to catch.

9. Lessons for an IAM specialist

  • A single realm with careful client/role separation is often the right call over multiple realms — realm boundaries are expensive to operate and should be reserved for genuine isolation needs, not reached for by default whenever two populations differ.
  • Toxic combinations are fixed structurally (mutually exclusive roles at provisioning time), not just detected after the fact — but detection (access review) still matters, because structural fixes don't catch drift introduced outside the original provisioning flow.
  • Real authorization requirements rarely fit one model. Expect to stack RBAC, ABAC, and sometimes ReBAC, and recognize the specific property (a role fact, a computed limit, a graph relationship) that tells you which model a given requirement actually needs.
  • Step-up authentication is a targeted tool, not a blanket policy — the skill is identifying which specific actions justify the friction, not maximizing MFA everywhere.
  • Least privilege applies as strictly to service accounts and automation as to human employees — an "in case we need it later" grant is a real, auditable finding, not a harmless convenience.
  • A revocation API call succeeding is not the same as the thing it was meant to do actually having happened — always verify the end state, not just the response code, for anything security-critical.
  • Compliance frameworks overlap. Design for the actual set of applicable obligations (here, PCI-DSS plus likely SOC 2 plus possible GDPR), not whichever single framework is most prominent.
