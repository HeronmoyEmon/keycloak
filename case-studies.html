Case studies

Three organizations run through every chapter of this course as the recurring, concrete example behind every scenario, quiz question, and lab: Northwind Apps, a B2B SaaS company; Riverside Health, a hospital network; and Meridian Trust, a retail bank. Chapter 2 introduces all three. Part X closes the course by returning to each one and designing its full IAM architecture end to end — a synthesis of everything the preceding 45 chapters taught, worked through as a real specialist would actually do the job.

Key idea

Each case study has two parts: a slide-deck chapter (chapters 46-48, same format as every other chapter — quiz, glossary, self-check included) that walks through the design narrative and recalls which earlier chapters each decision draws on, and a full long-form write-up below, built as a single reference page rather than a lesson — the kind of document you could actually hand to an interviewer or a new teammate.

Northwind Apps — multi-tenant SaaS IAM design

A B2B SaaS company serving thousands of customer tenants, from single-founder startups to Acme Corp-scale enterprises with their own identity provider. The design question: how do you give every tenant real isolation without paying realm-per-tenant's operational cost at scale, while still supporting enterprise SSO, self-service CIAM, and resource-level sharing decisions users make for themselves?

Riverside Health — HIPAA patient portal with step-up auth

A hospital network with two very different populations under one roof: patients who need a self-service portal, and clinicians who need fast, auditable access to records on shared clinical workstations. The design question: how do you satisfy HIPAA's minimum-necessary standard with a relationship that Keycloak has no native way to express — "is this clinician currently on this patient's care team" — while still leaving a deliberate, audited emergency path for the cases the standing rule can't anticipate?

Meridian Trust — PCI-DSS banking app, fraud-aware auth

A retail bank balancing two risk profiles under one PCI-DSS-scoped identity program: an internal workforce whose transfer-creation and transfer-approval roles must stay mutually exclusive, and retail customers whose authentication strength needs to scale with a transaction's actual fraud risk rather than apply uniformly. The design question: where does RBAC stop being enough, and what does each additional model — ABAC, then ReBAC — buy that the layer below it structurally can't?
