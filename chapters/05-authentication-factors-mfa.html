5. Authentication Factors & MFA

"Enable MFA" is one-line advice that hides a surprising amount of nuance: which factors, combined how, resistant to which attacks, and asked for how often? This chapter opens up that one line — the three classical factor categories, how a one-time code is conjured out of thin air without ever being transmitted in advance, why a hardware key or passkey resists phishing in a way a typed code cannot, and how modern systems decide to ask for more proof only when the situation actually looks risky.

Why this matters

An IAM specialist is routinely asked to pick an MFA strategy under real constraints: a hospital floor where clinicians can't fumble with a phone between patients, a bank that must stop a fraudulent wire transfer without annoying every legitimate login, a SaaS company whose enterprise customers demand phishing-resistant authentication in their security questionnaire. Getting this wrong in either direction — too little friction and credentials get phished, too much and people route around the control entirely — is one of the most consequential day-to-day decisions in the job.

Recall — chapter 1

Chapter 1 defined multi-factor authentication in one line: requiring more than one proof of identity, typically something you know plus something you have or are. That's correct but incomplete — it doesn't say which combinations actually resist which attacks, how a one-time code is generated without being sent over the wire in advance, or why some "second factors" (a typed code) are far weaker against phishing than others (a hardware key). This chapter builds the rest of that picture.

Factor categories: knowledge, possession, inherence — and now, context

Every authentication factor falls into one of three classical categories, each defined by what kind of proof it offers and, just as importantly, what it's vulnerable to losing:

The three classical factor categories
CategoryProof offeredTypical examplesWhat it loses to
Knowledge factorSomething you know Password, PIN, security question Phishing, guessing, reuse across sites, shoulder-surfing
Possession factorSomething you have Phone with an authenticator app, hardware security key, badge, SIM Theft, loss, SIM-swap (for SMS specifically), cloning (for weak possession tokens)
Inherence factorSomething you are Fingerprint, face, iris, voice Spoofing of a low-quality sensor, and — unlike a password — can't be rotated if compromised

A growing fourth category doesn't fit the "factor" framing cleanly but matters just as much: context and behavioral signals — the device being used, its location, the network it's on, typing rhythm, time of day. These aren't proof that credential-checking relies on directly; they're evidence about the situation that a system uses to decide whether the factors it already has are enough, or whether to ask for more. That decision is risk-based authentication, covered later in this chapter.

Pitfall

Two passwords is not MFA, even if one is "the account password" and the other is "a PIN" — both are knowledge factors, so compromising the thing that makes passwords weak (phishing, reuse, guessing) compromises both at once. Genuine MFA requires factors from different categories, so that defeating one doesn't automatically defeat the other.

Case study — Riverside Health

Riverside Health's clinicians authenticate to shared clinical workstations with a badge tap — a possession factor, often paired with a short PIN. The entire design optimizes for speed: a clinician moving between patients can't afford a typed password and a six-digit code every time. The trade-off is explicit and accepted: a lost or borrowed badge is a real risk, and badge-tap systems lean hard on short session timeouts, workstation-level controls, and audit logging (chapter 3) to compensate for the weaker guarantee a single possession factor gives on its own compared to a true multi-factor combination.

In Keycloak

Chapter 30 configures exactly this: Keycloak's Authentication flows let you require an OTP credential alongside a password, and its credential types cover WebAuthn and passkeys as registered credentials on a user. The admin console's Required Actions (e.g. "Configure OTP") is where a user is first enrolled in a second factor.

OTP mechanics: TOTP and HOTP, conceptually

The most common possession-factor implementation is the six-digit code from an authenticator app. It feels like magic — the app and the server agree on a number neither one just sent the other — but the mechanism is a straightforward idea: at enrollment, the server and the app establish a shared secret once (often via a QR code), and never transmit it again. From then on, each side independently combines that secret with a moving factor — something both sides can compute without communicating — through a one-way function, and the two results match if and only if both sides hold the same secret. The exact cryptographic function (an HMAC) is chapter 16's territory; what matters here is the shape of the trick.

The two standard variants differ only in what the moving factor is:

TOTP vs. HOTP
Moving factorCode changes whenMain weakness
TOTP (Time-based One-Time Password)Current time, rounded to a step (commonly 30 seconds)Every time step elapses, automatically Requires both sides' clocks to stay reasonably in sync
HOTP (HMAC-based One-Time Password)A counter that increments each time a code is usedOnly when a code is actually consumed Server and device counters can drift out of sync if a code is generated but never used
Pitfall

If a user's TOTP codes "stop working" after their phone's clock drifts or after a time-zone change, the cause is almost always clock skew between the device and the server, not a corrupted secret — most TOTP validators tolerate a small window of drift precisely because of this. Resetting the shared secret is rarely the right fix; resyncing the device's clock usually is.

WebAuthn, FIDO2, and passkeys: phishing-resistant by construction

A typed OTP code is still something a user can be tricked into typing into the wrong place. If an attacker stands up a lookalike login page, a victim who types in their password and a one-time code hands the attacker both — and the attacker replays them against the real site within the code's short validity window. This is a real, common attack pattern, and it's why "has MFA" and "is phishing-resistant" are not the same claim.

WebAuthn is a web standard (from the W3C) for public-key-based authentication built into browsers; FIDO2 is the broader set of standards it's part of, covering how a browser talks to an authenticator device. A passkey is the user-facing name for a FIDO2/WebAuthn credential designed to sync across a person's own devices via their platform account, so it feels like "just logging in" rather than "carrying a security key." Under the hood, all three rest on the same idea: at registration, the device generates a public/private key pair. The private key never leaves the device (often protected further by a secure hardware element); the public key is handed to the server. At login, the server sends a challenge; the device signs it with the private key, and critically, that signature is cryptographically bound to the exact origin (domain) that issued the challenge.

That origin-binding is the whole point: a lookalike phishing domain cannot obtain a valid signature for the real site's origin, no matter how convincingly it copies the page, because the signing operation itself refuses to run for the wrong origin. There is nothing for the user to be tricked into typing or copying — the secret that matters (the private key) never leaves the device and is never something a human sees or handles at all.

Worth knowing, not memorizing precisely

WebAuthn and FIDO2 are active, evolving standards — exact capabilities (e.g. which passkey syncing providers are supported, platform-specific behavior) change over time. Treat the mechanism described here — origin-bound public-key signatures, private key never leaving the device — as the durable concept; verify current specifics against the W3C/FIDO Alliance sites in the deep dive section when it matters for a real decision.

Risk-based (adaptive) authentication and step-up authentication

Asking for a second factor on every single login, forever, is one extreme; never asking is the other. Risk-based authentication (also called adaptive authentication) sits between them: a system evaluates contextual signals — is this a known device? a usual location? is the action being attempted sensitive? — and raises or lowers how much proof it demands accordingly. When a session that already passed normal login is asked for one additional factor specifically because of an elevated-risk moment, that's step-up authentication: not a replacement for the original login, an extra check layered on top of it, triggered by risk rather than by a fixed schedule.

Case study — Meridian Trust

Meridian Trust's online banking lets a customer log in, check balances, and review statements with their normal factor combination — password plus a registered device signal is usually enough, because none of that is especially damaging if something is slightly off. The moment that same customer initiates a wire transfer, the risk calculation changes: a large, irreversible transfer is exactly the sensitive action that justifies step-up authentication. Meridian's system evaluates signals (is this a new payee? an unusual amount? a device never seen on this account before?) and, if any combination crosses its threshold, interrupts the transfer flow to demand an additional factor — commonly a push approval or OTP — before the funds move. A customer transferring $20 to a payee they've paid monthly for two years from their usual phone sees no extra prompt at all; the same customer attempting a first-time $20,000 transfer from an unrecognized browser does, and that difference is the entire point of risk-based auth rather than a flat "always require MFA for wire transfers" rule, which would frustrate the first case for no security benefit.

Common mistakes

  • If you see two factors from the same category (two passwords, or a password plus a security question) being called "MFA," the cause is a misunderstanding of what makes multi-factor authentication strong — it's the different categories, not the raw count of prompts.
  • If you see a user locked out claiming their "OTP secret must be corrupted" right after a trip or daylight-saving change, the cause is almost always clock drift on a TOTP device, not a broken secret.
  • If you see a security questionnaire marking SMS one-time codes as equivalent to a hardware security key, the cause is treating "has a second factor" and "is phishing-resistant" as the same property, when SMS and typed OTP codes remain phishable in a way origin-bound WebAuthn signatures are not.
  • If you see every single action in an application demanding a fresh MFA prompt, the cause is usually a missing risk-based layer — treating all actions as equally sensitive instead of reserving step-up for the ones that actually warrant it, which trains users to click through prompts without reading them.
  • If you see "biometric login" described as something that can be "reset" the way a password can, the cause is forgetting that inherence factors can't be rotated the way a credential can — a compromised fingerprint template is a very different kind of incident than a leaked password.

Glossary terms introduced here

Takeaways

  • The three classical authentication factor categories are knowledge, possession, and inherence — genuine MFA combines two or more from different categories, not just two prompts.
  • Context and behavioral signals (device, location, typing pattern) aren't a traditional factor; they feed risk decisions about whether to demand more proof.
  • TOTP and HOTP both work by having two sides independently combine a shared secret with a moving factor — the secret itself is established once at enrollment and never transmitted again.
  • TOTP's moving factor is time (vulnerable to clock drift); HOTP's is a counter (vulnerable to counter desync if a generated code is never used).
  • WebAuthn is the browser-level public-key authentication standard; FIDO2 is the broader standard family it belongs to; a passkey is the user-facing, syncable form of a FIDO2/WebAuthn credential.
  • WebAuthn/passkey signatures are cryptographically bound to the exact origin that issued the challenge, which is precisely why they resist phishing in a way typed OTP codes do not.
  • Risk-based (adaptive) authentication raises or lowers the bar based on contextual signals instead of applying a flat rule to every login.
  • Step-up authentication is an extra factor demanded mid-session, triggered by an elevated-risk signal (a sensitive action, an unfamiliar device) rather than requested on a fixed schedule.
  • Possession-factor choices involve real trade-offs — Riverside Health's badge tap optimizes for clinical speed at the cost of weaker guarantees than a full multi-factor combination.
  • Meridian Trust's wire-transfer step-up shows risk-based auth in a concrete, worked form: routine actions pass with the normal factor set, unusual high-value actions trigger an additional check.
