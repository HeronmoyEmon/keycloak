    48. Case Study: Meridian Trust

    Meridian Trust is this course's retail-bank running example: PCI-DSS-regulated, fraud-pressured, and carrying two very different identity populations — bank employees and retail customers — on the same Keycloak realm. This chapter doesn't teach a new concept; it pulls together everything Parts I-IX built, one decision at a time, into a single coherent IAM architecture a specialist could actually defend to an auditor.

    Why this matters

    Nobody hires an IAM specialist to recite what RBAC or OIDC is in the abstract. They hire you to look at a specific organization — its compliance obligations, its threat model, its awkward edge cases — and make concrete, defensible decisions: which realm layout, which roles, which policy engine, which lifetimes. This chapter is that exercise end to end for Meridian Trust, reusing every decision this course's labs already made for it (chapters 20-45) rather than inventing a new scenario. The full write-up, with complete configuration tables and a decision log, lives in case-studies/cs03-meridian.html — this chapter is the guided walkthrough of it.

    Meridian Trust: who they are, what's at stake

    Chapter 2 introduced Meridian Trust as a retail bank with two identity populations: bank employees — tellers, loan officers, back-office staff, and wire-transfer approval staff, each with a different authorization need — and retail customers who bank online and through a mobile app. Chapter 11 pinned down why that distinction has legal teeth: Meridian's online banking platform touches payment card data, which puts a defined Cardholder Data Environment (CDE) inside PCI-DSS's scope (the current standard, PCI-DSS v4.0.1, per chapter 45). PCI-DSS is mandatory because Meridian processes card payments; chapter 11 also flagged that Meridian almost certainly carries a SOC 2 obligation on top of it (enterprise banking partners won't sign without one) and possibly GDPR exposure if it has EU retail customers or staff — real organizations rarely map to exactly one framework.

    What concept is this?

    Everything in this chapter is an instance of ideas Parts I-III built vendor-agnostically: AAA (chapter 3), authorization models (chapter 6), SSO/federation (chapter 7), and the IAM threat landscape (chapter 9) — applied here as concrete Keycloak configuration, not restated from scratch.

    Chapter 9 gave Meridian a real incident to design around: a spike in failed logins traced not to a password-hashing weakness at Meridian, but to an unrelated retail-site breach whose leaked passwords a fraction of Meridian's own customers had reused. Meridian's hashing and storage were never in question — the exposure was password reuse, and the control that actually limited the blast radius was the step-up authentication this chapter builds out in detail below, not a stronger password policy alone.

    Realm & client architecture: one realm, deliberately

    Chapters 20-30's hands-on labs ran their realm/role/client mechanics against northwind-apps as the concrete example — the mechanism is organization-agnostic, as chapter 20 itself says explicitly, so the identical steps with meridian-trust in place of northwind-apps produce the same result. Chapter 36 is the one chapter that actually ran those steps against a real meridian-trust realm directly (its service-to-service lab), rather than only discussing Meridian as a case-study tie-in. Either way, this architecture section's point holds regardless of which lab created which realm: a single realm, not split employees/retail realms. That's a real architectural choice, not a default: a realm is Keycloak's unit of complete isolation (separate users, roles, clients, themes, keys — chapter 21), so the alternative — meridian-employees and meridian-retail as two realms — was genuinely on the table.

    One realm won for Meridian specifically because employees and retail customers never need to be authentication-isolated from each other the way, say, two unrelated tenants would (chapter 21's multi-tenancy discussion) — they need to be authorization-isolated, which realm roles, groups, and client scopes already do well within one realm, at a fraction of the operational cost of running two realms' worth of themes, brute-force policies, and key rotation in lockstep. The separation that actually matters is enforced at the client and role layer instead.

    In Keycloak

    This is exactly the realm created in chapter 20's lab and extended in chapters 23/24: Realm settings > meridian-trust, with employee- and customer-facing applications registered as separate Clients, employee access modeled via Groups and Realm roles, and the fraud pipeline's client configured as a confidential client with Service accounts roles enabled, holding only view-users (chapter 36).

    Authorization: RBAC baseline, enforced by SoD, refined by ABAC

    Chapter 10 defined Meridian's clearest toxic combination: a single identity holding both transfer-creator (can create a wire transfer) and transfer-approver (can approve one) — each permission is routine alone, but together they let one person originate and be the sole check on the same fraudulent transfer, which is exactly PCI-DSS Requirement 7's need-to-know restriction made concrete (chapter 11). Chapter 23 operationalized the fix as a real Keycloak lever: transfer-creator and transfer-approver are modeled as mutually exclusive realm roles, assigned via separate groups, so no single provisioning action can silently create the toxic pairing.

    RBAC alone still can't express "how much" — chapter 6's worked example has Meridian layer ABAC on top of roles: a teller's role says they can approve transfers at all, but the actual dollar ceiling they can approve is computed from attributes (the teller's seniority tier and the account's risk tier), not from the role itself. Two tellers with the identical transfer-approver role can have very different approval ceilings, because the ceiling is an attribute computation, not a role fact.

    Pitfall

    Treating a toxic combination as checked once at grant time and done is the mistake chapter 10 warns against by name: role assignments drift (a loan officer temporarily covers wire-transfer duty during a staffing gap) and the combination can form silently later, with no single provisioning action looking wrong in isolation. Meridian's access-review cadence (chapter 10's IGA territory, layered on top of Keycloak since Keycloak itself has no SoD-conflict detection) is what catches this — not Keycloak's authorization engine alone.

    Case study — Meridian Trust

    Put together: a wire transfer at Meridian passes a role check (is this person a transfer-creator or transfer-approver, never both on the same transfer chain), then an attribute check (is the amount within this specific approver's computed ceiling for this account's risk tier). Two different authorization models, stacked, each doing the job the other can't.

    Where it genuinely runs out: the manager-of relationship

    Chapter 38 pushed Meridian's authorization model one step further and found a real boundary: an employee may approve a transaction only if they are the manager of the specific branch that owns the account. This isn't an attribute (an employee's profile doesn't encode which branches they manage), and it isn't a static role (a role says "this person is a manager," not "manager of this branch"), and it changes as managers rotate between branches. It's a genuine relationship edge between two specific entities — chapter 6's ReBAC definition, for real this time, not a hypothetical.

    Chapter 38 walked through the honest trade-offs of approximating this in Keycloak natively — a group per branch, or a delimited attribute string — and concluded both degrade the same way as the relationship gets more dynamic. Meridian's realistic end state, past a certain branch count, is a relationship store (OpenFGA or an equivalent Zanzibar-derived engine) holding manager_of and owns tuples, queried by a custom Keycloak Policy provider at approval time. Keycloak keeps authenticating Alex and issuing the token that proves who Alex is — it simply stops being the system of record for which branch Alex manages, because that was never really an authentication-system fact.

    Step-up authentication: routine banking vs. a wire transfer

    Chapter 5 drew the line that shapes Meridian's whole authentication posture: logging in to check a balance is low-stakes and gets Meridian's normal factor combination (password plus a registered device signal); initiating a wire transfer is high-stakes and justifies step-up authentication mid-session. Meridian's system evaluates contextual signals — is this a new payee? an unusual amount? a device never seen on this account before? — and, only if that combination crosses a threshold, interrupts the transfer flow to demand an additional factor (a push approval or OTP) before funds move. A $20 transfer to a payee paid monthly for two years from the usual phone sees no extra prompt; a first-time $20,000 transfer from an unrecognized browser does.

    In Keycloak

    Mechanically, this is a conditional step in an authentication flow (chapter 25): a Condition - User Role or Condition - User Attribute execution gates an OTP or WebAuthn execution, evaluated only when the client application (here, the wire-transfer endpoint, not the balance-check endpoint) requests step-up via acr_values/claims, or the resource server itself calls back into Keycloak for a fresh, stronger token before authorizing the transfer. The underlying second factor — password plus a one-time code — is the same AAA worked example chapter 30 used for Meridian's realm-wide MFA requirement; step-up reuses that mechanism, applied conditionally rather than on every login.

    Chapter 30 also fixed Meridian's realm-wide password and brute-force posture as compliance evidence, not abstract hygiene: a password policy requiring length, mixed case, digits, and special characters, plus notContainsUsername specifically (Meridian can't skip this one), and a brute-force detection policy tuned stricter than Keycloak's own defaults on failureFactor — but deliberately stopping short of permanent lockout, because a banking customer base is also a realistic denial-of-service target: an attacker who can lock out legitimate customers by deliberately failing their logins has found a different attack, not a safer system.

    Sessions and tokens: ending a stolen session, for real

    Chapter 29 gave Meridian a concrete incident to design session policy around: a teller's laptop is reported stolen mid-shift, still logged into Meridian's internal banking console. The chapter's point, reinforced by actually running the fix against a live Keycloak instance, is that a 204 response from a logout or revocation call only means the server accepted the request — proving the session is genuinely gone requires the second half of the runbook: enumerate the user's active sessions, issue a targeted DELETE /admin/realms/meridian-trust/sessions/ {sessionId} (or POST users/{id}/logout for all of them), and then verify by re-listing sessions that none remain for that device, rather than assuming the first call worked.

    Case study — Meridian Trust

    The teller in chapter 29's scenario had two active sessions (two devices) when the laptop theft was reported. Revoking only the laptop's session, confirmed by re-listing, is the targeted fix; revoking the teller's account wholesale would have logged them out of their own phone too, with no security benefit — exactly the discipline an auditor checking PCI-DSS Requirement 10 (log and monitor access) would expect Meridian to demonstrate on request.

    Service-to-service: scoping the fraud-detection pipeline down

    Chapter 36 is Meridian's clearest example of least privilege applied to a non-human identity. Meridian's nightly fraud-scoring pipeline — never a browser session, never a human operator, running unattended on a schedule — pulls account and user metadata out of Keycloak to feed a risk model. Before that chapter's fix, its client held manage-users "just in case a later feature needed write access": textbook over-provisioning (chapter 9), and exactly the kind of finding a PCI-DSS assessor reviewing least-privilege controls flags on sight. The fix, proven rather than assumed in that chapter's lab: a fraud-detection-service confidential client whose service account holds view-users alone — the pipeline still completes its actual job, and a write attempt now fails loudly and immediately, long before an assessor has to ask.

    In Keycloak

    Clients > fraud-detection-service > Service accounts roles, granting only the view-users client role from the realm-management client — not manage-users — authenticated via the client_credentials grant (chapter 13/36), with no interactive login step at all.

    Common mistakes and pitfalls

    Pitfall

    If you see a bank-style case study assign exactly one compliance framework ("Meridian = just PCI-DSS"), the cause is treating compliance as a single label instead of mapping the actual populations of data and people against every applicable framework — chapter 11's point, restated here because it's an easy simplification to reach for under time pressure.

    Pitfall

    If a design applies step-up authentication to every login rather than only to high-risk actions, the cause is treating "more MFA" as inherently safer without weighing the frustration cost against the actual risk — chapter 5's whole argument for risk-based auth over flat MFA rules.

    Pitfall

    If Keycloak's authorization engine is expected to detect a toxic combination or a stale access grant on its own, the cause is a scope confusion between IAM (chapter 1) and IGA (chapter 10/12) — Keycloak enforces whatever rules you configure; it has no governance layer watching for drift.

    Glossary

    Takeaways

    • Meridian Trust's whole IAM architecture is one realm, isolated by client and role rather than by realm boundary — realm separation is for true isolation needs, not every population split.
    • A toxic combination (transfer-creator + transfer-approver) is fixed by making the roles mutually exclusive at the provisioning layer, not by trusting reviewers to catch it later.
    • RBAC answers "can this person act at all"; ABAC answers "within what limit"; ReBAC answers "on whose specific accounts" — Meridian needs all three, stacked, not one chosen model.
    • The manager-of-branch relationship is a genuine ReBAC requirement Keycloak's native primitives can only approximate — the honest fix is a purpose-built relationship store, not a cleverer policy.
    • Step-up authentication is risk-based, not blanket: Meridian's normal login stays low-friction, and only a wire transfer's contextual risk signals trigger an extra factor.
    • A credential-stuffing incident traced to password reuse elsewhere, not a Meridian-side weakness, is exactly the scenario step-up auth is designed to contain the blast radius of.
    • Ending a stolen session safely means a targeted per-session revocation, re-verified by re-listing sessions — a 204 response alone doesn't prove the session is gone.
    • Least privilege applies to non-human identities too: Meridian's fraud pipeline needed view-users, never manage-users, and over-provisioning it was a real, assessor-visible finding.
    • Real organizations carry overlapping compliance obligations (PCI-DSS, likely SOC 2, possibly GDPR) — treating Meridian as "just a PCI-DSS company" understates its actual compliance surface.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first.

    1. Why did Meridian Trust end up with one realm instead of separate realms for employees and retail customers, given that a realm is Keycloak's unit of complete isolation?

      Model answerBecause employees and retail customers don't need authentication-level isolation from each other — they need authorization-level isolation, which realm roles, groups, and client separation already provide within one realm. Splitting into two realms would duplicate themes, brute-force policy, and key rotation for no corresponding security gain, since the actual separation that matters (who can create vs. approve a transfer, who can access banking vs. employee tooling) is enforced at the client/role layer, not the realm layer.

    2. A new hire is granted both transfer-creator and transfer-approver "temporarily" to cover a staffing gap. Using Meridian's own toxic-combination definition, explain exactly what's wrong and why a role-level fix alone isn't sufficient going forward.

      Model answerThe combination is toxic because transfer-approver exists specifically to be an independent check on transfer-creator — holding both lets this person originate a fraudulent transfer and be its own sole approver, erasing the check. Making the roles mutually exclusive at grant time stops it from being created going forward, but it doesn't catch drift (a role added later, outside the original provisioning flow) — that requires an ongoing access-review process, chapter 10's IGA point, layered on top of Keycloak rather than expected from it.

    3. Explain, in Meridian's own terms, why RBAC and ABAC together still can't express the manager-of-branch requirement, and name the specific property that makes it a ReBAC problem.

      Model answerRBAC can say "this person is a manager," but not of which specific branch. ABAC can encode an attribute like a department or tier, but an employee's profile doesn't naturally encode "manages branches X and Y" in a way that supports multi-hop traversal (manager of the branch that owns this account) or changes cheaply as managers rotate. The defining property is that it's a relationship between two specific entities (a person and a branch, then a branch and an account) that must be traversed, not a static fact attached to either entity alone — exactly what a relationship-tuple store is built to query and a role/attribute field is not.

    4. A colleague proposes requiring step-up MFA on every single login to Meridian's online banking, arguing "more security is always better." Using chapter 5's framing, what's the actual cost of that choice, and what does Meridian do instead?

      Model answerBlanket MFA on every login frustrates the overwhelming majority of low-risk logins (checking a balance from a known device) for no corresponding security benefit, since there's no elevated risk signal to justify the extra friction. Meridian instead applies step-up conditionally, triggered only when contextual signals on a specific high-stakes action (a wire transfer: new payee, unusual amount, unfamiliar device) cross a risk threshold — preserving low friction for routine use while still raising the bar exactly when it matters.

    5. Why does the fraud-detection-service case matter even though the service "was never going to intentionally write data anyway"?

      Model answerIntent isn't what least privilege defends against — a compromised credential, a bug, or a future code change could all turn an over-provisioned manage-users grant into real unauthorized writes, and a PCI-DSS assessor evaluating least-privilege controls judges the grant itself, not the operator's stated intent. Scoping the service account to view-users means a write attempt — accidental or malicious — fails immediately and loudly, rather than silently succeeding because the access happened to be there "just in case."
