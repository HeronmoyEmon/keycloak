    46. Case Study: Northwind Apps

    Every earlier chapter introduced Northwind Apps one concept at a time: a realm here, a role model there, a brokered enterprise customer in another. This chapter does the job an IAM specialist actually gets hired to do — pull all of that into one coherent design, defend the decisions, and name the trade-offs out loud. It's a synthesis chapter, not a new-concept chapter: nothing here is new material, but assembling it end to end is itself the skill.

    Why this matters

    "Design IAM for a multi-tenant B2B SaaS company" is a real assignment, not a classroom exercise — it shows up as a design doc, an architecture review, or an interview whiteboard question. The person who can recite "use RBAC" or "use Keycloak" in isolation fails that assignment; the person who can explain why Northwind rejected realm-per-tenant, why its token lifetimes moved in two different directions in the same change, and what specifically failed when one of its customers got breached, passes it. This chapter is that explanation, built entirely from decisions this course already made and lived with across chapters 2-45.

    Who Northwind Apps is, in one place

    Northwind Apps is a B2B SaaS company selling a multi-tenant dashboard product to other businesses (chapter 2). It is squarely a CIAM buyer (chapter 12): thousands of end-customer logins spread across many tenant companies, a bursty, consumer-scale traffic pattern, and a growing demand from its larger customers for enterprise SSO into its own product. Northwind also runs its own internal Active Directory for its employees — kept deliberately separate from the customer-facing identity store (chapter 4), because "who can log into Northwind's own dashboard product" and "who works at Northwind" are different populations answering different questions. Architecturally, Northwind's CIAM traffic pattern is exactly why it fits a containerized-to-Kubernetes deployment target (chapters 19, 42) rather than a fixed footprint.

    What concept is this?

    Every decision in this chapter is one of three things from earlier in the course applied to a real design: an authorization model choice (chapter 6), a federation or brokering choice (chapter 7), or a Keycloak-specific implementation of one of those choices (Parts IV-VIII). The job of this chapter is naming which is which, every time.

    Decision 1: one shared realm, not a realm per tenant

    Keycloak's realm is a fully isolated namespace (chapter 21) — a tempting unit to map one-to-one onto "one tenant." Northwind considered this directly and rejected it. A realm per tenant would give each customer company its own completely walled-off namespace of users, clients, and flows — strong isolation, on paper. But Northwind has thousands of tenants, not three: thousands of realms means thousands of separate admin-console contexts to manage, role definitions duplicated identically in every one of them, and no way to change a password policy or an authentication flow for "all tenants" without scripting the same change thousands of times over the Admin REST API (chapter 27) — the operational cost grows linearly with tenant count, forever.

    Instead, Northwind runs one realm, northwind-apps — the same realm every Keycloak lab chapter in this course has built against — and tags every customer-company user with a tenant_id custom claim, injected via the Protocol Mapper SPI implementation built in chapter 39. Tenant isolation becomes an authorization-time guarantee ("does this role assignment's scope match the request's tenant_id?") rather than a Keycloak-realm-boundary guarantee — a deliberate trade: slightly weaker platform-level isolation, in exchange for one realm that scales to Northwind's actual tenant count without linear admin overhead.

    Pitfall

    The mistake this trades away from is treating "one role name is assigned" as sufficient — chapter 6's resource-scope lesson applies directly. A tenant-admin role check that doesn't also verify which tenant the role was granted for is a cross-tenant privilege-escalation bug waiting to happen, not a theoretical one.

    Decision 2: enterprise customers get brokered SSO, not shared passwords

    Northwind's larger prospects routinely ask: "can our employees log in with their own corporate identity instead of a Northwind password?" (chapter 5). Chapter 7 named the two possible answers — federation (reuse an external directory directly) or brokering (delegate a single login event to an external IdP) — and chapter 31 built the real one: Northwind is the SP/RP, trusting northwind-corporate-idp as a stand-in for an enterprise customer's own identity provider. Northwind's Keycloak realm never touches that customer's directory; it only trusts an assertion.

    Case study — Northwind Apps

    Before chapter 31's fix, onboarding an enterprise customer like Acme Corp meant Northwind's ops team manually created a tenant-admin account (acme-admin) and repeated that by hand for every new Acme hire and every departure. After brokering Acme's own IdP, a brand-new Acme engineer (ben.carter) got a working northwind-apps identity automatically on first login — JIT-provisioned (chapter 2's "federated/brokered creation" lifecycle entry point) — with zero manual steps on Northwind's side, and Acme's own IT department stays the single source of truth for who currently counts as an Acme employee.

    Decision 3: per-tenant RBAC, with UMA delegation layered on for shared resources

    Chapter 6 named per-tenant RBAC as Northwind's primary model, specifically because "what job does this user have within their own tenant" is a stable, role-shaped question as long as every role assignment carries its tenant scope with it (the resource-scope discipline from the previous slide). But a pure role check can't answer every question a SaaS product needs answered — chapter 37's lab built the case directly: a tenant's own users sometimes need to share one specific document with one specific other user, a decision the document's owner makes at runtime, not something an admin encodes as a role ahead of time.

    Chapter 38's broader comparison is the right frame for explaining this to a skeptical reviewer: Northwind's per-tenant RBAC is a role-shaped answer; UMA's resource-owner delegation is closer to ReBAC in spirit — "did the person who controls this resource grant this relationship" — even though Keycloak implements it via UMA 2.0's permission tickets rather than a Zanzibar-style relationship graph. Naming which model is actually answering which question, out loud, is the skill chapter 6 and chapter 38 exist to build.

    Decision 4: a client per integration shape, not one client for everything

    Chapter 24's lesson — register the client that matches the actual integration, don't force one client type to do several jobs — shows up concretely across Northwind's realm.     In Keycloak

    All of this lives under one realm's Clients list in the Admin Console, each client's Capability config toggling exactly the grant types it actually needs (chapter 24) — turning on a grant a client doesn't use is a real, avoidable attack-surface increase, not a convenience.

    Decision 5: assume a customer's integration will have a bug

    Chapter 9's case study is Northwind's own cautionary tale: an enterprise customer's own browser-based integration had an XSS flaw Northwind never wrote and couldn't control. The attacker who exploited it could read that customer's bearer token — but the blast radius was bounded entirely by choices Northwind made ahead of time: how long that token lived, and how narrow its scope was. A SaaS provider inherits real risk from customer-side code; the controls below are what keep that risk from being unbounded.

    Chapter 21's own Northwind case study already shows this trade-off being made deliberately, in two directions at once: SSO Session Idle was lengthened (a B2B dashboard's users work in long, low-stakes stretches, and constant re-auth trains them to distrust the product) while SSO Session Max was shortened (an absolute ceiling on how long any one session, idle or not, can possibly stay valid) — tightening the control that bounds worst-case exposure while loosening the one that was only ever about day-to-day friction.

    Common mistakes

    Pitfall

    If a design doc says "we use RBAC for multi-tenancy" with no mention of resource scope, the cause is almost always an unexamined assumption that a role name alone is enough — it never is once two tenants share one realm. Ask explicitly: "scoped to what?"

    Pitfall

    If someone proposes realm-per-tenant "for better isolation" without being asked how many tenants exist today and in three years, the cause is evaluating isolation in isolation (so to speak) — every isolation decision has an operational cost that has to be weighed against the actual tenant count, not treated as free.

    Pitfall

    If a postmortem for a customer-side breach blames "the customer's bug" and stops there, the cause is skipping the harder question: what did our own token lifetime and scope choices do to the blast radius? Chapter 9's whole point is that the attacker's bug and the provider's blast-radius choices are two separate, independently assessable things.

    Glossary

    Takeaways

    • Northwind Apps is a CIAM buyer: thousands of tenant-company end-customers, bursty signup/login traffic, and growing enterprise-SSO demand from its larger customers.
    • Northwind rejected realm-per-tenant: strong isolation on paper, but operational cost that grows linearly with tenant count, which doesn't scale to thousands of tenants.
    • Its chosen design is one shared northwind-apps realm with a tenant_id custom claim, moving tenant isolation from a platform boundary to an authorization-time check.
    • A role check that doesn't verify its own resource scope (which tenant) is a cross-tenant privilege-escalation bug waiting to happen, not a hypothetical one.
    • Enterprise customers get brokered SSO (Northwind as SP/RP trusting the customer's own IdP), not federation and not shared passwords — chapter 7's brokering/federation distinction, applied.
    • Per-tenant RBAC answers "what's this user's job"; UMA resource-owner delegation (chapter 37) answers a different question, "did the owner of this specific thing choose to share it" — Northwind needs both, not one instead of the other.
    • Every client in the realm exists because of one distinct, real integration shape (human browser, machine-to-machine job, CLI with no browser) — not because one client type was reused for everything.
    • Blast radius from a customer-side bug Northwind never wrote (the XSS incident) was bounded by choices Northwind made in advance: token lifetime, token scope, and exact redirect_uri matching.
    • Session-lifetime decisions can tighten one control while loosening another in the same change, deliberately — lengthening idle timeout for UX while shortening the absolute session ceiling for risk.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first.

    1. A colleague proposes giving every Northwind tenant its own Keycloak realm "for clean isolation." What's the strongest argument against that, given Northwind's actual scale?

      Model answerIsolation quality isn't the deciding factor here — operational cost at scale is. With thousands of tenants, a realm-per-tenant model means thousands of admin contexts, duplicated role definitions, and any realm-wide policy change (a password policy, an authentication flow tweak) has to be scripted and applied thousands of times instead of once. The shared-realm-plus-tenant_id design accepts a weaker platform-level isolation guarantee specifically to avoid that linear operational cost.

    2. Why is "Northwind is the SP/RP trusting Acme's IdP" the correct description of the Acme relationship, rather than "Northwind federates with Acme's directory"?

      Model answerFederation means directly querying or binding against an existing external directory (the way Riverside Health's clinicians are federated against the hospital's AD). Northwind never touches Acme's directory at all — it configures a trust relationship with Acme's IdP and lets that IdP perform the actual authentication, then accepts the resulting assertion. That's brokering, chapter 7's distinction, not federation.

    3. Northwind's billing API has both a client-credentials client and a direct-grant client with two different user roles. Why not just use one client and one role for everything?

      Model answerThey represent genuinely different callers: the client-credentials client is a nightly job with no human and no resource owner at all, while the direct-grant users are real people who should be limited to exactly what their job needs — read-only (billing-reader) versus read/write (billing-admin). Collapsing them into one client and one role would either over-privilege the reconciliation job or under-privilege a real admin, and would make an audit log unable to distinguish "a human did this" from "the nightly job did this."

    4. In the Acme XSS incident, was Northwind's own application code at fault? What does that distinction change about the right response?

      Model answerNo — the XSS flaw was in code Acme's integration team wrote, not Northwind's. That distinction matters because it reframes the right response: Northwind can't fix a bug in someone else's code, so the only levers actually available to Northwind are the ones that bound the damage a stolen token can do — its lifetime and its scope — which is exactly why those are the controls this chapter frames as Northwind's responsibility, independent of whose bug caused the theft.

    5. Per-tenant RBAC and UMA's resource-owner delegation both get used inside the same request path in Northwind's design. Why isn't one of them redundant?

      Model answerThey answer different questions. Per-tenant RBAC answers "does this user generally hold a role that permits this kind of action within this tenant" — a static, job-shaped question. UMA's permission-ticket check answers a dynamic, per-resource question that no role assignment could encode ahead of time: "did the specific owner of this specific document choose, at some point, to share it with this specific other user." A request can pass the first and still need to pass the second if it targets a resource someone else owns and hasn't shared.
