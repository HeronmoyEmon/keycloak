3. AuthN vs. AuthZ vs. Accounting

Chapter 1 gave you one sentence each for authentication and authorization so you could move on. This chapter slows down and takes them apart properly, adds the third letter of the classic "AAA" model — accounting — and shows exactly how conflating the first two causes real, exploitable bugs, not just sloppy vocabulary.

Why this matters

An astonishing share of real-world access-control vulnerabilities trace back to one root cause: a developer checked "is this person logged in?" when the actual question was "is this specific person allowed to do this specific thing?" That single confusion — authentication standing in for authorization — shows up constantly enough that the OWASP Top 10 and countless bug-bounty writeups treat it as its own category ("broken access control"). An IAM specialist has to be able to spot that confusion in a code review, in a design doc, or in a Keycloak policy, before it ships. This chapter builds the vocabulary and the instinct to catch it.

Recall from chapter 1

Chapter 1 put it this way: Authentication (AuthN) answers "who are you?" Authorization (AuthZ) answers "what are you allowed to do, now that I know who you are?" Everything below is that same distinction, taken from a one-sentence summary to something you can actually apply — including the two-letter acronym you'll see constantly in logs, specs, and product docs: AuthN and AuthZ.

The formal AAA model

"AuthN vs. AuthZ" is usually taught as a pair, but the field's formal model has three parts, not two, going back to network-access-control standards like RADIUS and the broader discipline they popularized: Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting — commonly shortened to AAA. Each answers a different question about an identity's interaction with a system:

The three parts of the AAA model
PartQuestion it answersExample decision or action
AuthenticationWho are you?Verifying a password, OTP, or hardware key proves this session belongs to user jane.doe
AuthorizationWhat are you allowed to do?Checking whether jane.doe specifically has permission to approve this invoice
AccountingWhat did you actually do, and when?Recording that jane.doe approved invoice #4821 at 14:02 UTC from IP 203.0.113.9

Accounting is the part most beginner explanations of IAM skip entirely, which is why chapter 1 only forward-pointed to it rather than defining it. In plain terms: accounting is the act of recording what identities did — every login attempt, every permission check, every administrative change — as structured, timestamped events, usually as they happen. It is not optional bookkeeping; it's the raw material every later security investigation, compliance report, and anomaly-detection system depends on. Keycloak's own event-logging subsystem (full depth in chapter 28) is a concrete implementation of exactly this third "A."

Accounting vs. audit: related, not identical

Chapter 1 introduced audit trail as "a consistent, centrally recorded log... that lets an organization answer 'who accessed what, and when.'" That definition is actually describing the output of accounting — the recorded events. Audit, more precisely, is the separate activity of reviewing that output after the fact: a compliance team reading three months of access logs to confirm no one viewed records they shouldn't have, or an incident responder reconstructing what an attacker did during a breach window. Put simply:

  • Accounting is the recording act, happening continuously, in real time, as events occur.
  • Audit is the review act, happening later, often on a schedule (quarterly compliance review) or triggered by an incident.
Pitfall — terminology ambiguity, named honestly

"Accounting" is not used consistently across the industry, and it's worth being upfront about that rather than asserting one precise meaning everyone agrees on. In classic network-access-control contexts (RADIUS, the original AAA framework, dial-up and VPN access control) "accounting" often carries a connotation closer to its literal sense — metering usage for billing or capacity purposes (how many minutes connected, how much bandwidth used), not just security logging. In general modern IAM usage — the sense this course and most contemporary IAM writing uses — "accounting" has broadened to mean recording security-relevant events for audit and forensic purposes, with the billing connotation mostly gone. Some practitioners skip the word "accounting" entirely and just say "logging" or "audit logging" for this third A. If you see "accounting" used in a RADIUS-flavored document and expect it to mean "security event logging," double-check the source — it may be narrower than that.

Seeing AuthN and AuthZ as genuinely separate decisions

The single most common way beginners misjudge access control is treating "logged in" as a proxy for "allowed." The two really are independent axes — a system can get either one right or wrong independently of the other. A 2x2 view makes this concrete:

Authentication and authorization as independent outcomes
AuthZ correctAuthZ wrong
AuthN correctJane logs in as herself, views her own payslip — both decisions rightJane logs in as herself (correctly!), but the app lets her open any patient's chart by guessing a URL
AuthN wrongAn attacker guesses a weak password, logs in as Jane, but a tight permission check still blocks the one action they try — AuthZ saved the dayAn attacker logs in as Jane via a stolen password and the app has no real permission checks — both decisions wrong

The top-right cell is the one that trips people up, and it's worth a fully worked example. Picture Riverside Health's patient portal. A patient, Maria, authenticates correctly — her password and MFA code both check out, no doubt about who she is. She then navigates to /patients/8841/chart, which happens to be her own record. Out of curiosity, she edits the URL to /patients/8842/chart. If the application's only check was "is there a valid session," that request succeeds — she is shown a different patient's chart entirely, despite having authenticated flawlessly. Authentication was never the problem here; the system simply never asked the authorization question, "does this authenticated identity have a care relationship with patient 8842?" This exact failure pattern has a name in the web-security world — insecure direct object reference (sometimes shortened IDOR) — because it's common enough to be cataloged on its own.

A second concrete example, this time from Northwind Apps' multi-tenant SaaS product: an engineer at Acme Corp (one of Northwind's customer tenants) authenticates normally into the Northwind app. The product correctly confirms she is who she claims to be. But if the backend's authorization logic checks only "does this user belong to some paying tenant" rather than "does this user belong to this specific tenant whose data is being requested," she could potentially view a different customer's dashboard just by changing a tenant identifier in an API call. Again: perfect authentication, absent or wrong authorization.

Case study — Meridian Trust

Meridian Trust's online banking correctly authenticates a retail customer with a password plus a one-time code — strong AuthN. But a teller-facing internal tool at Meridian has a separate, sharper authorization question: a teller who is authenticated as a Meridian employee should not be able to open every customer account in the bank, only accounts tied to transactions they're actively handling, with every view logged for later review. Strong authentication at the front door says nothing about whether the right internal authorization boundaries exist once someone is inside — which is exactly the kind of control PCI-DSS auditors look for (full compliance depth in chapter 11).

Spot the mistake: two worked AuthN/AuthZ bugs

Below are two deliberately simplified, code-like pseudo-logic snippets. Both compile, both "work" in the sense that a developer who wrote them probably tested the happy path and moved on — and both are the exact confusion this chapter is about.

Bug 1 — checking "logged in" instead of "allowed to do this"
Buggy logicWhat's actually wrong
function getPatientChart(request) {
  if (isLoggedIn(request.session)) {
    return db.getChart(request.params.patientId);
  }
  return 403;
}		This function performs a flawless authentication check (isLoggedIn) and then treats it as sufficient to serve any patient's chart. It never checks whether the logged-in identity has a care relationship with patientId. Authentication answered "who are you," but the code used that answer to also decide "what can you access" — a question it never actually asked.
Bug 2 — checking the wrong scope of permission
Buggy logicWhat's actually wrong
function approveInvoice(request) {
  if (hasRole(request.user, "employee")) {
    invoices.approve(request.params.invoiceId);
    return 200;
  }
  return 403;
}		This one does perform an authorization check — but it checks the wrong, overly broad thing. "Is an employee" is true for almost the entire company; the real question is "is this specific employee authorized to approve this specific invoice" (right department, right approval limit, not the same person who created it). This isn't an AuthN/AuthZ mix-up like Bug 1 — it's a correctly-shaped authorization check answering the wrong question, which is just as exploitable.
  • If an endpoint checks isLoggedIn/isAuthenticated as its only access control, the cause is almost always that authentication was mistaken for authorization — add a specific permission or ownership check.
  • If a bug report says "User A could see User B's data by changing an ID in the URL," the cause is a missing object-level authorization check (an IDOR), not an authentication failure — the attacker was often fully, legitimately logged in as themselves.
  • If a role check passes but the action still feels "too broad" (any employee, any member, any tenant user), the cause is usually a role granularity problem — the check exists, but it's authorizing at the wrong scope (chapter 6 covers how RBAC, ABAC, and finer-grained models address exactly this).
  • If "we have audit logs" turns out to mean "we have web server access logs," the cause is conflating generic infrastructure logging with deliberate AAA-style accounting of identity and permission events — the two overlap but are not the same thing.

Chapter 5 goes deep on the mechanics of authentication itself — factors, MFA, WebAuthn/passkeys, and risk-based authentication — and chapter 6 goes deep on the mechanics of authorization — RBAC, ABAC, ReBAC, and the other models for deciding "what can you do" precisely instead of broadly. Nothing in this chapter tries to anticipate either; the goal here is only to make sure you never again let one stand in for the other.

In Keycloak

Keycloak keeps authentication and authorization as genuinely separate subsystems: authentication flows live under Authentication in the admin console, while who-can-do-what lives under a realm's Roles/Groups and, for fine-grained cases, a client's Authorization tab (UMA-based policies). Every login and administrative action is recorded in Keycloak's event system, viewable under Realm settings → Events — the accounting half of AAA, made concrete. Full depth on configuring and exporting that event data is chapter 28.

Case study: accounting and audit under compliance pressure

Both of this course's regulated-industry orgs depend on accounting producing a trail that audit can later use — for different legal reasons.

Case study — Riverside Health

HIPAA's Security Rule requires covered entities to maintain access accounting: the ability to produce, on request, a record of who accessed a given patient's record, when, and (often) why. If a patient asks "who at Riverside has looked at my chart in the last year," Riverside must be able to answer from its accounting data — not reconstruct a best guess after the fact. This is accounting in its purest sense: real-time recording that exists specifically so a later audit (or a patient's direct request) can be answered precisely.

Case study — Meridian Trust

PCI-DSS (the Payment Card Industry Data Security Standard) requires organizations handling cardholder data to track and monitor all access to network resources and cardholder data — concretely, logging who accessed what system, what action was taken, and reviewing those logs regularly. For Meridian Trust, that means every teller action on a customer account, every administrative change to a payment system, and every failed login attempt needs to land in an accounting trail that Meridian's compliance team can later audit — and that an external PCI assessor can later inspect. Chapter 11 covers PCI-DSS's IAM-relevant requirements in full; the point here is narrower: without rigorous accounting happening continuously, there is nothing for that later audit to review.

Takeaways

  • AAA is a three-part model, not two: Authentication (who are you), Authorization (what can you do), and Accounting (what did you do, recorded as it happens).
  • Accounting is the real-time recording act; audit is the separate, later activity of reviewing what accounting recorded — chapter 1's "audit trail" definition was really describing accounting's output.
  • "Accounting" carries a narrower, usage-metering connotation in classic RADIUS/network-access contexts than it does in general modern IAM usage — be alert to which sense a source is using.
  • Authentication and authorization are independent axes: a system can get either one right or wrong without the other being affected.
  • A perfectly authenticated user can still trigger a serious authorization failure — the Riverside Health "edit the URL to see another patient's chart" scenario (an IDOR) is the canonical example.
  • Checking isLoggedIn as a stand-in for a real permission check is the single most common AuthN/AuthZ conflation bug, and it is directly exploitable.
  • A correctly-shaped authorization check can still be wrong if it checks too broad a scope (e.g. "any employee" instead of "this specific employee, for this specific invoice").
  • Regulated industries turn accounting from a nice-to-have into a legal requirement: HIPAA expects Riverside Health to produce access-accounting records on request; PCI-DSS expects Meridian Trust to track and monitor cardholder-data access continuously.
  • Keycloak keeps authentication, authorization, and accounting as separate subsystems (Authentication flows, Roles/Groups/Authorization, and Events respectively) — full depth starting in chapters 5, 6, and 28.
