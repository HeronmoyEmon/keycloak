    10. Identity Governance & Administration (IGA)

    Chapter 2 gave you a generalized identity lifecycle — create, activate, modify, suspend, delete — and two lifecycle-stage terms, Provisioning and Deprovisioning, that both explicitly promised "the formal governance process around this is chapter 10's subject." This is that chapter. Provisioning and deprovisioning are the mechanics of granting and removing access. Governance is the discipline that keeps asking, for as long as an identity exists: is the access it currently holds still the access it should hold — and who is answerable for that being true?

    Why this matters

    Every identity that has existed for more than a few months has almost certainly accumulated access it no longer needs — a project it finished, a team it left, a temporary exception nobody revoked. None of that is caused by a provisioning bug. It's the predictable, structural result of organizations constantly changing while access, once granted, tends to stay granted unless something actively goes looking for it. An IAM specialist who only knows how to create and disable accounts is managing half the problem. The other half — catching accumulated access before an auditor, a regulator, or an attacker finds it first — is identity governance and administration, and it's the subject every compliance framework in chapter 11 will assume you already understand.

    Recall — chapter 2's identity lifecycle

    Chapter 2 defined a vendor-agnostic lifecycle every identity moves through: create → activate → modify → suspend → delete. That lifecycle is the thing this chapter governs — it doesn't get replaced or re-derived here. Everything below, especially Joiner-Mover-Leaver, is a different name laid on top of those same five stages, not a competing model.

    What IGA actually is

    Identity Governance & Administration (IGA) is the discipline of provisioning, reviewing, and governing access over an identity's entire lifetime — making sure access is granted correctly, stays correct as circumstances change, and is provably correct when someone asks. "Correct" has a specific, checkable meaning here: every entitlement an identity holds should be traceable to a legitimate business reason that still applies today, not just the reason that was true when it was granted. IGA is a process discipline first — the market of products that automate that process (SailPoint, Saviynt, and others) is a separate subject, covered as a tooling category in chapter 12. This chapter is about the process itself: what has to happen, regardless of which tool — if any — does it.

    In Keycloak

    Keycloak has no built-in access-review, recertification-campaign, or SoD-conflict-detection feature anywhere in its admin console. That's not an oversight to work around — it's a real, specialist-level distinction worth naming precisely: Keycloak is an IAM product (it authenticates, issues tokens, and enforces the authorization decisions you configure), not an IGA product (it doesn't run periodic campaigns asking "should Priya still have this role," and it doesn't know what a toxic combination is). Chapter 12 places Keycloak precisely on this market map against products that do govern access as their core job.

    Joiner-Mover-Leaver: the enterprise name for the lifecycle you already know

    Joiner-Mover-Leaver (JML) is the HR-driven vocabulary enterprises use for exactly the lifecycle chapter 2 already generalized. It isn't a new lifecycle to learn — it's a relabeling that groups the same five stages into three HR-recognizable events:

    • Joiner — maps onto create + activate. Someone joins the organization (or a new system within it) and gets an identity with its starting access.
    • Mover — maps onto modify. Someone changes role, team, or location within the organization, and their access needs to change to match.
    • Leaver — maps onto suspend + delete. Someone leaves the organization (or a role within it) and their access needs to end.

    Joiner and Leaver are the easy cases — most organizations get them right, because an obvious HR event (hire, termination) triggers an obvious IAM event (create an account, disable an account). Mover is where beginners consistently get it wrong, and it deserves a close look.

    Pitfall — the Mover trap

    A role change is not purely additive. When Northwind Apps moves a support engineer onto the platform engineering team, the naive implementation just grants the new team's access on top of whatever the person already had — because "modify" sounds like "add something," and adding is simpler than figuring out what to take away. The correct behavior: the mover event should trigger a review of the identity's entire current access, not only the addition of the new role's access. Chapter 6 established Northwind's RBAC as per-tenant and per-team scoped — a support engineer's role-scoped access to the support team's tooling and tenant-facing queues has no legitimate reason to persist once they're a platform engineer. If it does persist, that's not a feature, it's accumulated privilege nobody revoked — exactly the "stale access" an access review exists to catch.

    Case study — Northwind Apps' Mover flow

    Northwind Apps staff occasionally move between product teams — say, from the support team to the platform engineering team. A correctly designed Mover event does two things, not one: it grants the platform engineering team's role-scoped access and it removes the support team's role-scoped access the person no longer has a job-related reason to hold. Skipping the second half is the single most common JML mistake — the person ends up with the union of every role they've ever held instead of the role they currently hold, and nobody notices until an access review (next section) specifically goes looking for exactly this pattern.

    Access reviews and recertification

    An access review (also called recertification) is a periodic, deliberate check of who has access to what, specifically to catch access that accumulated but is no longer justified — the Mover-trap pattern above, access left over from a finished project, an exception granted "just for this week" eighteen months ago. Access reviews exist because provisioning and deprovisioning, even when both work correctly, only react to events someone remembered to trigger. Nothing in a purely event-driven system catches access that nobody remembered to revoke, because by definition no one noticed it needed revoking. A review is the deliberate, scheduled act of looking anyway.

    The access under review is usually broken into individual entitlements — a single, specific grant (one role, one group membership, one permission on one system) rather than "everything this person can do" as one undifferentiated blob. Reviewing entitlement by entitlement is what makes a review actionable: a reviewer can confirm "yes, this specific EHR-write entitlement is still correct" without having to re-justify every other thing the same person can do.

    Case study — Riverside Health's clinician access certification

    Riverside Health runs periodic access certification campaigns over clinician EHR access specifically because chart access, once granted for a rotation or a care-team assignment (chapter 6's ReBAC relationship), tends to outlive the clinical reason for it — a clinician rotates off a ward but keeps chart-read access to patients they no longer treat. This is precisely the kind of accumulated access the HIPAA Security Rule's minimum necessary standard is concerned with — access should be limited to what's needed for the current task, not everything a clinician has ever needed. Chapter 11 covers HIPAA's requirements formally; the point here is narrower: a recurring access certification is the operational mechanism that makes "minimum necessary" something you can actually demonstrate, rather than just assert.

    Access certification campaigns and attestation

    An access certification is the specific act, performed by a reviewer with the authority to judge it, of examining one entitlement and deciding whether to keep it or revoke it. A recertification campaign is the organized, scheduled exercise that bundles many individual certifications together — typically all of one manager's reports, or all access to one sensitive system, reviewed on the same cadence (quarterly and annual campaigns are both common) so the organization can point to a complete, dated record rather than an ad-hoc patchwork of individual checks. Attestation is the formal confirmation itself — the reviewer's recorded "yes, this access is still correct" (or "no, revoke it") for a specific entitlement, which is what turns a review from an informal glance into something an auditor can later verify actually happened.

    Pitfall — rubber-stamping

    A recertification campaign only works if the reviewer actually evaluates each entitlement against a real question ("does this person still need this, for a reason I can state?"). The common failure mode is rubber-stamping: a manager facing a list of forty entitlements for twelve direct reports, under a deadline, clicks "certify all" without checking any of them individually. The campaign still produces a dated, signed-off record — which is exactly what makes rubber-stamping dangerous: it looks indistinguishable from a real review in an audit log, while providing none of the actual security benefit. A review's value comes entirely from the judgment applied during it, not from the fact that a review nominally occurred.

    Segregation of Duties (SoD) and toxic combinations

    Segregation of Duties (SoD) — also called Separation of Duties — is the principle that no single identity should hold two permissions whose combination lets that one identity both carry out a sensitive action and be the only check on that same action. The control isn't about either permission being dangerous alone; a person who can create payment requests is fine, and a separate person who can approve payment requests is fine. The danger is one identity holding both, because that identity can then originate a fraudulent action and approve its own wrongdoing, with no independent party ever seeing it.

    A toxic combination is the specific pair (or set) of permissions that creates this condition. What makes a combination toxic is precise, not vague: it's the ability to both perform an action and be the sole check on that same action. "Can read customer data" plus "can read employee data" is not toxic by this definition — neither permission checks the other. "Can create a wire transfer" plus "can approve a wire transfer" is toxic, because the second permission exists specifically to check the first, and one identity holding both erases the check entirely.

    Case study — Meridian Trust's payment-approval SoD control

    Meridian Trust enforces SoD on its payment-approval chain as a direct fraud control: a teller who can create a transfer request must not also hold the permission to approve that same transfer. This is the textbook toxic combination — without the separation, a single compromised or dishonest teller identity could originate a fraudulent transfer and approve it themselves, leaving no independent party who ever had the chance to catch it. Meridian's access reviews specifically check for this combination across every teller identity, not just at the point either permission is granted — because a teller who legitimately held only "create" could later be granted "approve" for an unrelated reason (covering a vacationing colleague, say) and the toxic combination would form silently, with no single provisioning action looking wrong in isolation.

    When true separation isn't practical: compensating controls

    Small teams sometimes can't fully separate duties — a two-person back office may not have a third person available to be the independent approver. When separation itself isn't feasible, organizations use a compensating control: a different safeguard that substitutes for the missing separation, such as a mandatory secondary review by someone outside the team, an automatic flag on any transaction a toxic-combination holder both created and approved, or a hard dollar cap below which the conflict is tolerated and above which a true second approver is required. A compensating control is an explicit, documented trade-off — "we accept this specific risk because of this specific mitigating safeguard" — not a silent gap where the organization pretends the conflict doesn't exist.

    The underlying principle behind true separation is sometimes called the four-eyes principle (or dual control): a sensitive action requires two different people to look at it — one to perform it, one to independently verify it — before it takes effect. SoD is the organizational policy of assigning permissions so that four-eyes is structurally guaranteed rather than merely hoped for.

    Orphaned accounts: when deprovisioning and governance both miss

    An orphaned account is an account whose owning identity has left the organization (or a role) but whose access was never actually removed — deprovisioning that should have happened, didn't. Orphaned accounts are usually not caused by a missing process; they're caused by a missing trigger for an existing process — an offboarding step that depended on someone remembering to flip a switch, and nobody did. This is exactly what access reviews exist to catch as a backstop: even if the Leaver event that should have deprovisioned the account was missed entirely, the next scheduled review should surface "this identity's manager says they left four months ago, and this account is still active" as a finding, independent of whether the original offboarding trigger ever fired.

    Common mistakes

    • Treating a Mover event as purely additive — granting the new role's access without reviewing whether the old role's access should be removed (the Northwind trap above).
    • Running access certification campaigns on a schedule but letting reviewers rubber-stamp them without actually evaluating each entitlement.
    • Confusing "no single permission is dangerous" with "no combination of permissions is dangerous" — SoD is specifically about combinations, not individual grants.
    • Treating a toxic combination as fixed forever once checked at grant time, instead of re-checking it at every access review — combinations can form later, from two separately reasonable-looking grants.
    • Leaving a small team's unavoidable SoD gap undocumented, instead of recording it explicitly as a compensating control with its own safeguard.
    • Assuming Keycloak (or any pure IAM product) will surface stale access or SoD conflicts on its own — it enforces whatever access you've configured, but it doesn't ask whether that access is still justified.
    • Relying only on event-driven deprovisioning to catch every departure, with no periodic review as a backstop for the Leaver events that get missed and turn into orphaned accounts.

    Takeaways

    • IGA is the discipline of provisioning, reviewing, and governing access over an identity's whole lifetime — a process discipline, distinct from the IGA tooling market category chapter 12 covers.
    • Joiner-Mover-Leaver doesn't introduce a new lifecycle — it's the HR-driven vocabulary for chapter 2's create/activate/modify/suspend/delete stages, grouped into three events.
    • Mover is the event beginners get wrong most often: a role change should remove access tied to the old role, not just add access for the new one.
    • Access reviews (recertification) exist because accumulated, no-longer-justified access doesn't trigger any event on its own — only a deliberate, scheduled look catches it.
    • Segregation of Duties forbids one identity holding both the ability to perform a sensitive action and the sole check on that same action — a toxic combination is defined by that specific property, not by either permission being dangerous alone.
    • Access certification campaigns bundle individual entitlement reviews into a scheduled, dated exercise; attestation is the reviewer's recorded confirmation that makes the review auditable.
    • Rubber-stamping produces a campaign record indistinguishable from a real review while providing none of its security value — the benefit comes entirely from the judgment applied, not the record.
    • When true separation of duties isn't feasible, an explicit, documented compensating control is the honest alternative to a silent, unacknowledged gap.
    • Keycloak is an IAM product, not an IGA product — it has no built-in access-review, recertification, or SoD-conflict feature, a real and useful distinction rather than a gap to apologize for.
    • Orphaned accounts are the predictable result of a missed Leaver trigger; periodic access review is the backstop that catches them when event-driven deprovisioning alone doesn't.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first — the point is to check your own reasoning against it, not to read the answer first.

    1. Explain, without using the words "create" or "add," why a Mover event that only grants new access is incomplete. What specifically should it also do?

      Model answerA role change should also trigger removal of the prior role's entitlements, not merely a grant of the new role's entitlements — otherwise the identity ends up holding the union of every role it has ever occupied instead of the role it currently holds. The Mover event should prompt a review of the identity's entire current access and revoke whatever was tied specifically to the role being left, the way Northwind Apps should revoke a former support engineer's support-queue access once they move to the platform engineering team.

    2. Give an example of two permissions that are each individually harmless but form a toxic combination together. What specific property makes the combination toxic, not just the pairing itself?

      Model answerMeridian Trust's "create a wire transfer" and "approve a wire transfer" are each routine and safe on their own. The combination is toxic specifically because the approve permission exists to be an independent check on the create permission — one identity holding both lets it originate a fraudulent transfer and be its own sole approver, erasing the check entirely. It's that "perform the action and be the sole check on it" property that makes a pairing toxic, not simply that the two permissions happen to be related.

    3. Why can't event-driven provisioning and deprovisioning alone guarantee that an identity's access stays correct over time? What gap does a scheduled access review fill that no single event can?

      Model answerProvisioning and deprovisioning only react to events someone remembers to trigger — a hire, a termination, a role change. Access that accumulates with no corresponding event, like a finished project's leftover grant or an eighteen-month-old "just for this week" exception, has nothing to react to it, so nothing removes it automatically. A scheduled access review doesn't wait for a trigger at all; it deliberately re-examines every entitlement on a fixed cadence, which is the only mechanism that catches access nobody remembered needed revoking — the same gap Riverside Health's clinician certification campaigns exist to close for chart access left over after a ward rotation.

    4. A manager "certifies" all forty of their reports' entitlements in under two minutes. What's wrong with this, even though the campaign's record will show it as completed on time?

      Model answerThis is rubber-stamping: the audit record will show a completed, dated attestation, but no actual judgment was applied to any individual entitlement. A rubber-stamped campaign is indistinguishable from a genuine one in the log, which is exactly what makes it dangerous — a review's entire value comes from evaluating whether each specific entitlement is still justified, not from the fact that a certification was nominally performed on schedule.

    5. Why is "Keycloak doesn't do access reviews" a meaningful, specific statement about what kind of product Keycloak is — rather than just a missing feature to complain about?

      Model answerIt names a real category boundary rather than a bug: Keycloak is an IAM product — it authenticates identities, issues tokens, and enforces whatever authorization decisions you've configured — not an IGA product, which would run periodic recertification campaigns and detect SoD conflicts on its own initiative. Keycloak has no concept of "should Priya still have this role" or "is this a toxic combination"; governance has to be layered on top of it by process or by a separate IGA tool (chapter 12's subject), which is a deliberate scope distinction in the market, not an oversight Keycloak happens to have.

    6. Describe a compensating control you'd propose for a two-person team that can't fully separate the duties of creating and approving a sensitive action.

      Model answerSince a genuinely independent third approver isn't available, a workable compensating control is a mandatory secondary review by someone outside the team for any action one of the two people both created and approved, combined with an automatic flag that surfaces every self-approved transaction for later audit — or, alternatively, a hard dollar threshold below which the overlap is tolerated and above which an outside approver must sign off. Whichever safeguard is chosen, it should be documented explicitly as an accepted, mitigated risk rather than left as a silent, unacknowledged gap.
