    9. IAM Threat Landscape

    Everything built in chapters 3 through 8 — authentication, directories, MFA, authorization models, SSO and federation, tokens and sessions — is also, from an attacker's point of view, a surface to attack. This chapter closes Part I by walking through five threats every IAM specialist has to design against, and naming exactly where in this course the defense for each one lives, so the chapters still ahead read as answers to a problem you've already seen, not abstract best practice.

    Why this matters

    An IAM specialist isn't only the person who configures a login screen — they're the person an incident responder calls when a botnet is hammering the login endpoint with breached passwords, or when a phishing campaign has just harvested forty employees' credentials in an afternoon. If you can't say, specifically, which part of the authentication or session architecture a given attack exploits, you can't argue for the control that stops it, and you can't explain to a security auditor why a control is there at all. This chapter exists so that chapter 5's MFA, chapter 18's OAuth/OIDC hardening, chapter 29's session design, and chapter 30's Keycloak brute-force settings land later in this course as direct answers to named attacks, not as homework for its own sake.

    The other half of the story

    Chapters 3 through 8 built the vocabulary for how identity is supposed to work: the formal AuthN / AuthZ / accounting split (chapter 3), directories that store identities (chapter 4), the factors that prove who someone is (chapter 5), the models that decide what they're allowed to do (chapter 6), how one login spans many applications (chapter 7), and the tokens and sessions that carry proof of all of it around (chapter 8). Every one of those is also a place an attacker can push on:

    • AuthN/AuthZ (ch. 3) — credential stuffing and phishing both attack the authentication step directly; once AuthN is bypassed, a weak authorization model decides how much damage follows.
    • Directories (ch. 4) — a compromised directory account doesn't just leak a password, it hands the attacker everything that directory entry is trusted for.
    • MFA (ch. 5) — phishing and MFA fatigue exist specifically to defeat the extra factor, not just the password underneath it.
    • Authorization models (ch. 6) — least-privilege design is what limits the blast radius once an attacker is in, regardless of which threat below got them there.
    • SSO & federation (ch. 7) — the same property that makes SSO convenient (one session, many apps) makes a stolen SSO session dangerous: it's one theft away from access to everything that session was trusted for.
    • Tokens & sessions (ch. 8) — token theft, token replay, and session hijacking attack this layer directly, and the "revocation problem" that chapter introduced — a stateless bearer token can't easily be invalidated before it expires — is exactly why they're dangerous, not just theoretical.

    Credential stuffing

    Credential stuffing is the automated, large-scale reuse of credential lists leaked from other sites' breaches against a login form, betting that some fraction of users reused the same email/password pair. The attacker doesn't need to guess or crack anything — they already have real, working username/password pairs; the only open question is which of your users reused one of them. Attempted at the scale of millions of credential pairs through botnets and residential proxy networks, even a 0.1–2% hit rate (the typical range reported across public credential-stuffing incident write-ups) produces thousands of compromised accounts.

    In Keycloak

    A realm's Brute Force Detection settings — failure-count threshold, wait-time increment, permanent-lockout option — are exactly this defense, configured in chapter 30. Nothing is installed yet, but the knobs you'll turn later are a failure counter per account, a growing delay between retries, and an optional permanent lockout after repeated failures.

    Case study

    Meridian Trust notices a sudden spike in failed logins against its online banking portal — hundreds of thousands of attempts over a weekend, spread across thousands of IP addresses, each trying exactly one username/password pair before moving on. Fraud analytics later confirms the pattern: the credentials being tried match a password dump from an unrelated retail website breach, and a measurable fraction of Meridian's own customers had reused their banking password there. No password was cracked; Meridian's hashing and storage were never in play. The incident is contained by rate-limiting and lockout, but it motivates mandatory MFA for banking logins — the step-up authentication this course sets up for Meridian back in chapter 2.

    Phishing

    Phishing is tricking a user into handing over credentials — or approving a fraudulent MFA prompt — via a convincing fake login page, email, or message that impersonates someone the user trusts. Unlike credential stuffing, phishing doesn't need the user to have reused a password anywhere; it manufactures a brand-new, valid credential disclosure directly from the target. The more advanced variant, adversary-in-the-middle (AiTM) phishing, proxies the real login page live and relays whatever the victim enters — including a one-time MFA code — straight through to the real service in real time, which is why OTP-based MFA alone doesn't stop it.

    Case study

    Riverside Health clinicians receive an email that looks like it comes from the hospital's own IT help desk, warning that their "clinical systems account will be locked in 24 hours" unless they verify it through a linked page. The page is a pixel-for- pixel copy of the real single sign-on login screen, hosted on a lookalike domain. Several clinicians, juggling patient care and a plausible-sounding deadline, enter their credentials; a smaller number, prompted by the fake page's follow-up screen, also approve what looks like a routine MFA push. The attacker now holds valid credentials and, for the small number who approved the prompt, a live session — all without touching Riverside's actual infrastructure at any point.

    Token theft and replay

    Token theft is stealing a valid bearer token — through a client-side vulnerability, a misconfigured storage location, or an intercepted network request — and replaying it before it expires. Chapter 8 introduced the defining property of a bearer token: whoever holds it can use it, and a stateless token can't easily be invalidated before its natural expiry — the "revocation problem." Token theft is why that problem isn't academic: once a token is stolen, its lifetime is a countdown the legitimate owner usually doesn't control, and a long-lived token gives the attacker a correspondingly long window.

    Case study

    Northwind Apps — a B2B SaaS platform — learns that one of its enterprise customers built its own browser-based integration against Northwind's API, and that integration has a cross-site scripting (XSS) flaw in code Northwind never wrote and doesn't control. An attacker who exploits that flaw can read whatever that customer's browser session is holding, including the bearer token Northwind issued for API access. Northwind's own application code is never at fault, but Northwind's blast radius is: the stolen token is scoped to that customer's data and lives until it expires, during which the attacker can act as that customer's integration against Northwind's API. It's a clean illustration that a SaaS provider inherits real risk from customer-side code it doesn't write, and that the token lifetime and scope Northwind chose are what determine how bad someone else's bug gets to be.

    Session hijacking

    Session hijacking is stealing a session identifier or cookie — rather than a password or a bearer token — and using it to impersonate an already-authenticated session. The mechanism differs from token theft (a session ID looked up server-side versus a self-contained token), but the consequence is the same: whoever holds the identifier is treated as the legitimate user until the session is invalidated. Because SSO (chapter 7) deliberately makes one session span many applications, a hijacked SSO session can be more consequential than a single stolen password ever was — it's one theft away from everything that session was trusted for.

    In Keycloak

    Keycloak's per-realm session view lets an administrator revoke a user's active sessions on demand, and short default access-token lifetimes limit a stolen token's window — both covered in chapter 29. The broader OAuth 2.0 Security Best Current Practice guidance this course applies to Keycloak clients — sender-constrained tokens, PKCE-everywhere, safe token storage — is chapter 18.

    MFA fatigue / push bombing

    MFA fatigue, also called push bombing, is repeatedly sending MFA approval push notifications to a target — often late at night or in rapid bursts — until a tired, confused, or simply annoyed user approves one by mistake, assuming it's a glitch rather than an active attack. It requires the attacker to already have a valid password (often from credential stuffing or phishing); the push bombing is specifically how they get past the second factor once the first one is in hand.

    Case study

    At Meridian Trust, an employee whose password was already compromised in the same credential-stuffing wave described above starts receiving MFA push notifications at 23:58 — four of them in six minutes, well outside their normal login hours. Half- asleep and assuming a glitch in the banking app, they approve the fifth one just to make the notifications stop. The attacker, who had the password but not the second factor until that moment, is now signed in with full approval.

    In Keycloak

    Authentication flows can require a WebAuthn/passkey step instead of (or alongside) an OTP push — a factor that has nothing to approve by tapping "yes" and is correspondingly immune to fatigue-based bombing. Chapter 5 introduces why it's phishing- and fatigue-resistant; chapter 30 configures it.

    Pitfall

    It's tempting to read every incident above as a user-error story — someone reused a password, someone clicked a link, someone tapped "approve" half asleep. Resist that framing. In every case the organization had a system-level choice available that would have removed the user's ability to make that mistake at all (rate limiting, phishing-resistant MFA, short token lifetimes, session revocation). Blaming the user is a way of avoiding the control that was actually missing.

    • If login attempts fail silently with no lockout or slowdown, the cause is missing rate-limiting or brute-force protection, not bad luck — see chapter 30.
    • If MFA is OTP-only, the cause is a factor that adversary-in-the-middle phishing can relay in real time; WebAuthn/passkeys close that gap, OTP alone does not — see chapter 5.
    • If a stolen access token keeps working for hours or days after it's reported stolen, the cause is a token lifetime set too long with no practical revocation path, not an unavoidable fact of bearer tokens — see chapters 18 and 29.
    • If a push-bombed user eventually approves out of fatigue, the cause is an MFA method that allows unlimited, unrate-limited retries, not carelessness on the user's part.
    • If a SaaS provider treats a breach as entirely "the customer's fault" because the vulnerable code was the customer's, the cause is usually that the provider's own token scope and lifetime choices are what let that customer-side bug reach provider-side data at all.

    Takeaways

    • Every threat in this chapter targets a specific layer built in chapters 3-8 — none of this is random; attackers go after login, MFA approval, tokens, or sessions because that's where proof of identity actually lives.
    • Credential stuffing doesn't break passwords — it exploits password reuse across sites the victim organization never controlled.
    • Phishing increasingly targets the MFA approval step, not just the password, which is why OTP-based MFA is not phishing-proof the way WebAuthn/passkeys are.
    • A stolen bearer token or session identifier is just as dangerous as a stolen password — anyone holding it can act as the user until it's revoked or it expires.
    • The "revocation problem" from chapter 8 — a stateless bearer token can't easily be invalidated before expiry — is precisely why token theft is dangerous in practice, not just in theory.
    • MFA fatigue/push bombing exploits a UX decision (unlimited push retries), not a weakness in the user who eventually approves one.
    • A SaaS provider's blast radius can include vulnerabilities in a customer's own integration code it doesn't control, if the tokens it issues to that customer aren't scoped or short-lived enough to contain the damage.
    • Every threat here has a named defense taught later in this course — chapter 5, chapter 18, chapter 29, chapter 30 — this chapter is motivation, not a dead end.
    • Least-privilege authorization (chapter 6) limits how much damage a single stolen credential or session can do, even after authentication itself has been compromised.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first — the point is to check your own reasoning against it, not to read the answer first.

    1. Pick one threat from this chapter and trace it back to which of chapters 3-8's concepts it exploits, and explain why that concept being present is also what makes the attack possible.

      Model answerCredential stuffing exploits chapter 3's authentication (AuthN) concept directly: AuthN typically proves identity with a shared secret — a password — and because that same secret-based proof pattern exists on every site, a password leaked from one breach can be replayed against any other login form. The very thing that makes password-based AuthN work (a reusable secret the user remembers) is also what makes it attackable at scale the moment that secret leaks elsewhere; without password-based authentication as a concept, there would be nothing for an attacker to stuff.

    2. Why doesn't adding more OTP-based MFA prompts fully solve phishing? What category of authentication factor closes the gap, and why?

      Model answerAn adversary-in-the-middle phishing proxy, as this chapter describes, simply relays the real login page live — including whatever MFA challenge it issues — straight through to the victim and back. Adding more OTP prompts just gives the proxy more things to relay in real time; it doesn't make relaying harder. WebAuthn/passkey-based factors close the gap because the credential is cryptographically bound to the origin (domain) it was registered for — a passkey registered for the real login domain simply produces no valid signature when challenged by a lookalike domain, so there's nothing the proxy can relay that will actually work.

    3. Explain in your own words why a stolen bearer token is dangerous specifically because of the "revocation problem" introduced in chapter 8, not just because it's "stolen data."

      Model answerA bearer token is self-contained and stateless — the server doesn't check back with an issuing authority each time, it just trusts whoever presents the token. That's exactly why the revocation problem matters here: a stolen password still has to be presented at a login step that can be rate-limited or locked out, but a stolen token already looks like precisely the credential the API expects, and without revocation infrastructure it keeps succeeding until its own expiry timestamp — a countdown the attacker didn't set and the victim can't easily cut short. It's not just "stolen data"; it's a credential the system has no easy way to invalidate on demand.

    4. Northwind Apps' incident in this chapter wasn't caused by Northwind's own code. Should Northwind still bear responsibility for reducing the blast radius? Why or why not?

      Model answerYes, but with a clear line drawn. The XSS vulnerability itself is the customer's responsibility, since it lives in code Northwind never wrote and doesn't control. But the token's scope and lifetime are entirely Northwind's design decisions, and those choices are what determined how much damage that customer-side bug could do once exploited. Root-cause responsibility and blast-radius responsibility aren't the same thing — Northwind can't fix every customer's code, but it can (and should) make sure its own tokens are scoped narrowly and expire quickly enough that someone else's bug doesn't become Northwind's incident.

    5. Pick one row from the threat-to-defense table and argue whether that later chapter's defense alone would be sufficient, or whether it needs to be combined with another chapter's defense to actually stop the threat.

      Model answerTake MFA fatigue, whose primary defense is phishing-resistant MFA (chapter 5/30) — a factor with no approve/deny prompt to fatigue. That alone stops the push-bombing mechanism specifically, but it doesn't address how the attacker got a valid password in the first place, which is usually credential stuffing or phishing. If the compromised password is still valid and no rate-limiting or lockout exists (chapter 30's brute-force protection), the attacker denied the push vector can simply keep trying other entry points. Phishing-resistant MFA needs to be combined with brute-force protection and consistent MFA enforcement across every login path, not applied to just one flow, to actually close the loop.

    6. If you were advising Meridian Trust after its credential-stuffing wave, would you recommend an immediate forced password reset for all customers, mandatory MFA, or both — and what trade-off are you weighing in that choice?

      Model answerBoth, not either/or. A forced reset addresses the already-compromised passwords right now — it closes the window the credential-stuffing section describes, where the attacker already holds a working password. But reset alone is a one-time cleanup; customers can simply create new passwords that get reused again later, so it doesn't fix the underlying exposure. Mandatory MFA is the durable fix the case study itself ties to this incident, since it means a reused password alone is no longer enough to log in. The trade-off: an immediate reset creates real support-desk friction for every customer at once with no way to tell which of them were actually affected, and mandatory MFA adds ongoing friction to every future login for every customer — against the alternative of leaving a bank exposed to a known, already-exploited weakness if neither control is added.
