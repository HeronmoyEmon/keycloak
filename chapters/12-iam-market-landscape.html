    12. The IAM Market Landscape

    Chapter 1 used "IAM" as if it were one thing. It isn't. The moment you start shopping for a product, or interviewing for a role, the market splits into at least four distinct categories with different buyers, different vendors, and different jobs — and this chapter is where that split finally gets named properly, closing the loop chapter 1 opened and setting up the honest "does Keycloak fit here" question this course has been deferring since page one.

    Why this matters

    An IAM specialist is routinely asked questions like "should we buy Okta or build on Keycloak?" or "do we need a PAM tool on top of what we already have?" — questions that are unanswerable until you know the market has real seams in it, not just competing brand names for the same thing. This chapter gives you the taxonomy and the vendor map so those conversations go from guesswork to an informed trade-off discussion, and it gives you a live, dated snapshot of who owns what, because this is a market that reorganizes itself through acquisitions faster than most textbooks get reprinted.

    Recall from chapter 1

    Chapter 1 introduced two terms as one-liners and promised full depth here: Workforce IAM — "managing an organization's own employees and internal systems access" — and CIAM — "managing external, customer-facing identities." Chapter 1 also distinguished Keycloak from "commercial alternatives like Okta, Auth0, Microsoft Entra ID, and Ping Identity" without comparing them, promising that comparison for this chapter. Both promises get paid off below.

    The market has four seams, not one

    "IAM" as a job title, a budget line, and a market category is really four overlapping-but-distinct businesses. Knowing which one a given tool, vendor, or conversation is actually about is the single most useful piece of market literacy an IAM specialist can have — most confused procurement conversations trace back to two people silently assuming different quadrants of this diagram.

    Pitfall

    Treating "IAM" as one undifferentiated category is the single most common beginner mistake in this space. A vendor that is excellent at consumer-scale CIAM (millions of social-login signups) is not automatically good at workforce SSO into forty internal SaaS tools, and neither is automatically a substitute for a PAM tool that vaults and records privileged admin sessions. "We already have IAM" is not a complete answer to "do we have PAM" or "do we have IGA" — they are different purchases, often from different vendors, solving different problems.

    Workforce IAM, in depth

    Workforce IAM manages the identities of an organization's own people — employees, contractors, and the service accounts those people operate on behalf of — authenticating them into internal tools, SaaS business applications (email, CRM, ticketing), VPNs, and increasingly also cloud-provider consoles. Its defining shape is a bounded, known population: the number of identities roughly tracks headcount, every identity is typically backed by an HR record somewhere (feeding the joiner-mover-leaver flow chapter 10 covers), and the buyer is usually IT or security, not product. SSO into SaaS tools, directory federation (chapter 4/32), and role- or group-based authorization (chapter 6/23) are the day-to-day workforce IAM workload.

    CIAM, in depth

    CIAM (Customer Identity and Access Management) manages identities for people outside the organization — customers, end users, members of the public — who sign up for and log into a product the organization sells or operates. Its defining shape is the opposite of workforce IAM's: the population is unbounded and self-registering (anyone can sign up at 3am), scale is measured in the millions rather than the hundreds or thousands, social login and progressive profiling matter more than directory federation, and the buyer is usually the product organization, not IT. Login-page branding, conversion-rate impact of every added friction step, and consumer-grade uptime expectations are CIAM-specific pressures that workforce IAM rarely faces at the same intensity.

    PAM: a genuinely new category for this course

    PAM (Privileged Access Management) is the first of the four categories this course hasn't touched yet, and it deserves its own definition rather than a one-line mention. PAM manages the smallest, highest-risk slice of identities in any organization: the accounts that can do the most damage if compromised — database admins, root/domain-admin accounts, cloud-provider root accounts, break-glass emergency accounts, and the service accounts that back automation with broad permissions. A privileged account is any account whose normal operating permissions exceed what a typical end user holds — not every admin account is privileged in the PAM sense (a help-desk agent who can reset passwords for one department is a limited admin, not a PAM target; a domain controller admin is).

    PAM tools exist because workforce IAM's usual tools — SSO, MFA, RBAC — don't by themselves solve privileged access's specific risks. A PAM product adds:

    • Credential vaulting — privileged credentials (root passwords, database connection strings, SSH keys, API secrets) are stored in an encrypted vault rather than memorized or shared over chat; a human or script checks a credential out when needed instead of knowing it permanently.
    • Session recording — once a privileged session starts (an SSH session to a production database, an RDP session to a domain controller), the PAM tool records keystrokes and/or screen activity for later audit, the same way a bank's security camera records who walked into the vault, not just that the vault was opened.
    • Just-in-time (JIT) privilege elevation — instead of holding elevated permissions permanently (standing privileged access), a user requests elevation for a limited window and a specific task, and the permission is automatically revoked afterward — shrinking how long a compromised credential would actually be dangerous.
    • Break-glass access — a deliberately logged, alarm-raising emergency path to bypass normal elevation when something is on fire and the usual request-approval loop would be too slow; used rarely, audited heavily when it is used.
    • Routing privileged sessions through a bastion host (jump server) — a single hardened chokepoint all privileged connections must pass through, so there is one place to monitor, record, and, if necessary, cut off access, instead of admins connecting directly to sensitive systems from anywhere.

    Workforce IAM and PAM are complementary, not competing: workforce IAM answers "is this person who they say they are, and what role do they generally hold," while PAM answers the narrower, higher-stakes question "should this already-authenticated person be allowed to touch the most sensitive system right now, for this specific task, and is someone watching while they do it."

    IGA: referenced here, defined in chapter 10

    The fourth category, IGA (Identity Governance and Administration), is the governance and review discipline chapter 10 defines in full — access certification, segregation of duties, and the joiner-mover-leaver pattern. This chapter only needs IGA as a market/tooling category: a distinct class of products (SailPoint and Saviynt are the names that come up most often, discussed below) exists specifically to automate that governance discipline at scale, separate from the identity systems — Workforce IAM, CIAM, or Keycloak itself — whose access those products review. If you need IGA's actual definition, chapter 10 is where it lives; this chapter won't repeat it.

    In Keycloak

    Keycloak is a general-purpose IAM product that competently covers Workforce IAM and CIAM use cases — it has no dedicated PAM module (no credential vault, no session recording, no JIT elevation workflow) and no IGA module (no periodic access-certification campaigns, no SoD rule engine). That's not a flaw in Keycloak; it's a category boundary. An organization running Keycloak for workforce/CIAM identity may still need a separate PAM tool for its most sensitive admin accounts and a separate IGA tool (or process) for periodic access review — the same way it would alongside Okta, Auth0, or any other Workforce-IAM/CIAM product.

    The vendor landscape, live-verified as of November 2026

    Corporate ownership in this market changes fast enough that a vendor comparison written even a year earlier can already be wrong — this course's own content rules require live-verifying exactly this kind of fact rather than trusting memory. Here is what a live search turned up for each name in this table, as of this writing (2026-11-10), including the places the sources disagreed or hedged:

    • Auth0 and Okta — still the same corporate entity: Okta acquired Auth0 in 2021, and that hasn't changed. What has shifted is branding and internal structure: Okta's current flagship name for the product is "Okta Customer Identity Cloud, powered by Auth0" (also seen written as "Auth0 by Okta"), and Okta's own materials say both names refer to the same technology — expect to see both in the wild. Okta announced in April 2026 that it is unifying the Okta and Auth0 product/engineering organizations under one executive, with Auth0's own president departing as part of that reorg — an organizational integration, not a change in who owns the product.
    • ForgeRock and Ping Identity — ForgeRock was acquired by Thoma Bravo in 2023 and folded into Thoma Bravo's existing portfolio company, Ping Identity; that acquisition is complete and not in question. What is genuinely less settled, and worth saying honestly rather than guessing: how far the product-line merge has gone. Some sources describe ForgeRock's products as being progressively rebranded into the PingOne line; others describe the two platforms as still commercially distinct as of 2026, with existing ForgeRock Identity Platform deployments continuing to receive patches. This course can't resolve that discrepancy with confidence from search results alone — treat "ForgeRock" in this table as "now a Ping Identity brand/product line of uncertain current integration depth," and verify directly against Ping's own site before making a purchasing decision based on it.
    • Microsoft's identity product — still branded Microsoft Entra ID as of November 2026. It was renamed from Azure Active Directory (Azure AD) in 2023; nothing found suggests a further rebrand since. ("Azure AD" and "AAD" are the old names; Microsoft's own documentation now uses Entra ID throughout.)
    • IGA category leaders — SailPoint is the longer-standing incumbent, frequently cited (on somewhat dated revenue/market-share figures) as the category's largest vendor by revenue. Saviynt is the vendor most visibly gaining analyst recognition in 2026 specifically (named a leader across multiple dimensions in a 2026 KuppingerCole Leadership Compass for IGA, per Saviynt's own reporting of that analyst report). No neutral, up-to-date source in this search named one undisputed "market leader" for 2026 — both names are legitimate to know, and this course names both rather than picking a winner it can't actually verify.
    • PAM category leaders — including a fact worth a second look: Palo Alto Networks completed a roughly $25 billion acquisition of CyberArk in February 2026, and in May 2026 rebranded the CyberArk product line under the name Idira (visible directly on paloaltonetworks.com and reflected in CyberArk's own site). This is the single most consequential, easy-to-get-wrong fact in this chapter — "CyberArk" is still the name most practitioners use and search for, but the current corporate/product reality as of November 2026 is that it now sits inside Palo Alto Networks under the Idira name. BeyondTrust is the long-running, strongly analyst-rated runner-up in the category (named a leader in multiple 2026 analyst reports). Delinea also shows up credibly in 2026 analyst coverage. As with IGA, this course names the field rather than asserting one fixed "winner."

    Total cost of ownership: "free" isn't free

    The most consequential, specialist-level framing in this chapter is total cost of ownership (TCO): the full cost of running an identity system, not just its sticker price. Open-source vs. commercial IAM and self-hosted vs. SaaS/managed IAM are two related but separate axes worth pulling apart:

    • Keycloak carries no license fee and can be self-hosted anywhere — but "no license fee" is not "no cost." Someone has to run the Postgres/database backend, apply security patches, size and tune the deployment, handle clustering and failover (Part IX), and genuinely understand OAuth2/OIDC/SAML well enough to configure it correctly (Part III) — real, ongoing operational expertise, not a one-time setup task.
    • A commercial SaaS product (Okta, Auth0/Okta CIC, Entra ID, Ping) trades that operational burden for a recurring subscription cost, usually scaling with user count or monthly active users. The vendor runs the infrastructure, ships the patches, and is contractually on the hook for availability — at a price premium that grows with scale, and with less control over exactly how the system behaves or where data physically lives.
    • Data residency — where identity data is physically stored and which jurisdiction's laws govern it — is a real driver toward self-hosting for regulated organizations, independent of the pure dollar-cost comparison.
    • Vendor lock-in — the harder-to-quantify cost of how difficult it would be to leave a given product later (proprietary extensions, migration tooling quality, contract terms) — belongs in the same TCO conversation even though it rarely shows up on an invoice.

    Case study: which category would each org actually buy?

    Case study

    Northwind Apps is squarely a CIAM buyer: its whole business is a multi-tenant B2B SaaS product with thousands of end-customer logins across many tenant companies, consumer-scale signup/login traffic patterns, and a need for enterprise SSO into its own product for its larger customers — the exact shape CIAM vendors are built around.

    Riverside Health evaluates primarily self-hosted workforce IAM: its clinician workforce is the core identity population, and HIPAA-driven data-residency and control concerns (chapter 11) push it toward wanting its own infrastructure and full visibility into where identity data lives, rather than handing that control to a SaaS vendor — a real, defensible reason an organization might lean toward something like Keycloak specifically.

    Meridian Trust needs workforce IAM plus a PAM layer on top: everyday employee access to internal banking systems is a workforce IAM problem, but the small set of accounts with direct administrative access to core banking infrastructure is exactly the higher-risk, smaller-population problem PAM tooling exists for — a bank is a textbook case for not treating "we have workforce IAM" as a substitute for "we have vaulted, recorded, time-boxed privileged access to the systems that move money."

    Common mistakes

    • Assuming one vendor's strength in one category (e.g. consumer-scale CIAM) automatically means it's the right fit for a completely different category (e.g. workforce SSO or PAM) — these are different products solving different problems, often from different teams within the same vendor.
    • Comparing Keycloak's "free" license cost to a SaaS subscription without counting the operational expertise, infrastructure, and ongoing patching/tuning labor Keycloak requires — the comparison that matters is total cost of ownership, not license price alone.
    • Treating a corporate acquisition or rebrand (Auth0/Okta, ForgeRock/Ping, CyberArk/Idira) as settled forever rather than checking current status before a real purchasing decision — this market moves fast enough that a "fact" from even a year ago can already be stale.
    • Assuming "we have an IAM system" answers "do we have PAM" or "do we have IGA" — they are different purchases, not implied by having solved workforce or customer identity.
    • Forgetting that IGA (chapter 10) is a governance/process discipline, not only a piece of software — buying an IGA tool without the underlying review process and organizational buy-in just automates a process that doesn't actually exist yet.

    Back to chapter 1's question: when does Keycloak specifically make sense?

    Chapter 1 asked, vendor-agnostically, when to adopt an IAM system at all. With the market mapped out, that question narrows to something more specific and more honest: given that you've decided you need Workforce IAM and/or CIAM, when does Keycloak make sense versus a commercial alternative?

    Recall from chapter 1, narrowed

    Chapter 1's "reach for centralized IAM when" / "it's overkill when" framing still applies before this question even comes up. Assuming that bar is already cleared, the Keycloak-specific version of the question is: does this organization want control badly enough to accept the operational burden that control costs?

    Keycloak tends to fit well when: the organization has (or is willing to build) real operational capacity to run and patch a production service; data residency, regulatory control, or cost-at-scale make a per-user SaaS subscription unattractive; the team wants to customize deeply via SPIs (Part VIII) in ways a closed commercial product wouldn't allow; or the organization is already comfortable operating its own infrastructure (Kubernetes, databases, clustering) and sees Keycloak as one more well-understood workload rather than a novel burden.

    A commercial alternative tends to fit better when: the organization wants to outsource the operational burden entirely and is comfortable paying a premium for that; it needs extremely rapid time-to-value with minimal in-house protocol expertise; it needs enterprise support contracts and SLAs a vendor is contractually bound to; or its scale/consumer-facing needs point it specifically toward a CIAM-optimized product most commercial vendors have invested in more deeply than Keycloak has.

    Neither answer is "better" in the abstract — this is a genuine trade-off, not a sales pitch for either side, and the honest answer depends on an organization's existing operational muscle more than on any feature checklist. This course picked Keycloak as its hands-on vehicle because it's free to run yourself and because its openness makes it an excellent teaching tool for the protocols underneath — not because it's objectively the "best" IAM product for every organization reading this. Part III builds the protocol depth (OAuth2/OIDC/SAML) this decision assumes, and Part IV is where Keycloak itself finally gets installed and operated for real.

    Glossary terms introduced here

    Takeaways

    • "IAM" as a market splits into at least four distinct categories — Workforce IAM, CIAM, PAM, and IGA — with different buyers, different vendors, and different day-to-day workloads.
    • Workforce IAM manages a bounded, HR-backed population of an organization's own people; CIAM manages an unbounded, self-registering population of external customers at consumer scale.
    • PAM is the first genuinely new category in this chapter: it protects the small set of highest-risk privileged accounts via credential vaulting, session recording, and just-in-time privilege elevation — distinct from everyday authentication/authorization.
    • IGA is a governance/review discipline (chapter 10 owns its full definition) that also exists as a distinct market category of tooling, separate from the identity systems whose access it reviews.
    • Corporate ownership in this market changes fast: Auth0 remains part of Okta (branding evolving), ForgeRock is now inside Ping Identity (integration depth disputed in sources), Microsoft's product is still Entra ID, and CyberArk was rebranded Idira under Palo Alto Networks in 2026 — verify any of these again before relying on them in a real decision, since this market moves faster than most references get updated.
    • Total cost of ownership, not license price alone, is the right lens for comparing Keycloak to a commercial SaaS alternative — "free to license" and "cheap overall" are different claims.
    • Data residency and vendor lock-in are real TCO factors that don't show up on an invoice but belong in the same comparison as subscription cost and operational burden.
    • No single vendor dominates every category at once — a vendor's strength in one quadrant of the taxonomy says little about its fit for a different quadrant.
    • Keycloak fits organizations that want control and have (or will build) the operational capacity to run it; commercial alternatives fit organizations that would rather pay to outsource that operational burden — this is a genuine trade-off, not a case either side automatically wins.

    1. Explain, without naming Keycloak, why a vendor that's excellent at CIAM isn't automatically a good choice for a PAM need, even though both get called "IAM."
