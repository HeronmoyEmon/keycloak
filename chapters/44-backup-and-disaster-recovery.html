    44. Backup, Disaster Recovery & Multi-Region

    Chapter 41 made Keycloak survive one node dying. This chapter asks the harder question: what happens when the data is gone — a bad migration, a deleted realm, a destroyed database volume, or an entire data center going dark? Clustering keeps a healthy Keycloak available. Backup and disaster recovery is what gets you back when it stops being healthy at all, and multi-region is what happens when "one data center" itself is the single point of failure left to remove.

    Why this matters

    Every governance control from chapter 10 and every compliance framework from chapter 11 quietly assumes the data they govern still exists. An access review is meaningless if the identity store it reviews was never backed up. HIPAA's auditors don't just ask whether access is controlled — they ask what happens the day a server room floods. An IAM specialist who can configure roles and policies but has never tested a restore has only solved half of "available," and availability is one-third of the CIA triad this whole field quietly assumes.

    Sources for this chapter: Keycloak's own Importing and exporting realms guide and High Availability Guide, including its multi-cluster (v2) and multi-cluster (v1, deprecated) sub-guides — all fetched live and checked against the 26.8.2 release selector for this chapter — and 45 CFR §164.308(a)(7), the HIPAA Security Rule's Contingency Plan standard, read directly from Cornell LII's text of the regulation.

    What concept is this?

    Chapter 10 defined identity governance as the discipline of keeping access correct for as long as an identity exists — but "correct" only means anything if the records governance relies on (who has what role, who approved it, who's in which group) are still there to review. Chapter 11 then walked through compliance frameworks — GDPR, SOC 2, HIPAA, PCI-DSS — that all, in different words, demand proof that data and access controls survive an incident, not just that they exist today. This chapter is the "data survives" half those two chapters assumed and didn't explain. It's also the chapter chapter 41 named directly: its own lab deliberately proved cross-node session replication within one cluster and explicitly scoped out "anything about a real multi-region deployment," calling that out as "chapter 44's subject." This is that chapter — and its glossary already has a term, Single-cluster HA vs. multi-site HA, written specifically to hand off to it.

    Database backup vs. realm export: not the same tool

    It's tempting to treat Keycloak's built-in realm export feature as "the backup," because it's the thing with an obvious button and a JSON file to show for it. It isn't — and Keycloak's own documentation says so plainly. The database is Keycloak's actual source of truth. Every realm, client, user, credential, role, group, session row, and admin event Keycloak has ever written lives in its relational database (PostgreSQL in this course's labs). A real backup strategy backs up that — with your database vendor's own backup tooling (pg_dump, continuous WAL archiving, a managed database's automated snapshot feature) — not Keycloak itself.

    Realm export/import (kc.sh export / kc.sh import) is a narrower, different-purpose tool: a way to move realm configuration — and, if asked, users — between environments, independent of any particular database. It's what you'd use to promote a realm definition from a staging Keycloak to production, or to snapshot "how this realm is configured" for version control. It was never designed to be a disaster-recovery mechanism, and Keycloak's docs are explicit about several things it does not carry: user and admin events, persisted sessions, in-flight workflow state, and revoked token records. Restore a realm from an export after a real incident and you get the realm back — minus its audit history, its active sessions, and anything mid-flight when the export was taken.

    Pitfall

    If a team's only disaster-recovery artifact is a folder of realm export JSON files, they have a configuration snapshot, not a backup. A real incident response plan needs both: database backups (and tested restores) as the actual recovery mechanism, with realm export reserved for what it's genuinely good at — environment promotion and configuration review.

    RTO and RPO: naming the trade-off precisely

    Two terms from the broader disaster-recovery discipline (not Keycloak-specific, but essential for talking about any backup strategy precisely) frame every decision in this chapter:

    • Recovery Point Objective (RPO) — how much data you can afford to lose, measured as time: the gap between your last good backup and the moment of failure. Nightly backups mean an RPO of up to 24 hours; continuous WAL streaming to a standby can shrink that to seconds.
    • Recovery Time Objective (RTO) — how long you can afford to be down: the gap between failure and service being usable again. A manual restore-from-snapshot process might take hours; a warm standby that a load balancer can fail over to might take minutes.

    These are organizational decisions, not technical defaults — Keycloak's own high-availability guides are explicit that you have to decide and validate your own RTO/RPO targets; the product gives you building blocks, not a number. Tightening either one almost always costs more: more frequent backups, a standby database, a second region, more engineering and operational effort to keep it all tested and current.

    Case study — Meridian Trust

    A retail bank's core banking IAM (chapter 11's PCI-DSS territory) typically needs a tight RTO — every minute the authentication layer is down, tellers, ATMs, and the mobile app can't verify anyone. Its RPO tolerance for credential/role data is usually tighter still — losing even a few minutes of "who was granted fraud-review access" is itself an audit finding, independent of any actual fraud. That combination is exactly what pushes an organization like Meridian toward the costlier end of this chapter's options: frequent backups, a tested restore runbook, and — once clustering alone isn't enough — a second site.

    Multi-region: the options Keycloak's own docs currently describe

    Chapter 41 clustered two nodes within one site and explicitly deferred "a real multi-region deployment" to this chapter. Live-verified against Keycloak's current High Availability Guide, there are three documented shapes — and the guidance here is honestly thinner than chapter 41's single-cluster material: no failover runbooks, no latency thresholds, and an explicit statement that you must validate your own RTO/RPO rather than rely on a published number.

    Keycloak's documented multi-site topologies, live-verified against the current High Availability Guide
    TopologyTrade-off
    Single-cluster, multi-AZ/DC stretch — one Kubernetes/cluster deployment whose nodes happen to span availability zones or data centers with low enough latency; sessions live in the database, auth sessions in memorySimplest to operate, but the cluster's own control plane is still one logical unit — a control-plane failure can affect every node at once
    Multi-cluster v2 (current, recommended) — two or more fully independent Keycloak clusters, no shared Infinispan cluster, coordinated only through a synchronously-replicated database and an external load balancerTolerates a whole-cluster or AZ failure and needs no special networking between sites, but roughly doubles database CPU/write IOPS and needs sub-10ms cross-site commit latency
    Multi-cluster v1 (deprecated, scheduled for removal) — two clusters, each with its own Infinispan cluster, bridged across sitesCan satisfy regulatory demands for physically distinct deployments, but needs two full Kubernetes control planes, doesn't support three-plus AZs, and Keycloak's docs say to migrate off it
    Pitfall

    If you see multi-region Keycloak described as "just add a second cluster and point a load balancer at both," the cause is skipping the database. Every documented multi-site option requires a synchronously replicated database across sites — asynchronous replication risks stale reads and, per Keycloak's own docs, "data loss or an inconsistent state, including user sessions" on database failover. The load balancer is the easy part; the database topology is where the real engineering cost lives.

    Pitfall

    Don't assume a failed site gets automatically removed from rotation. Keycloak's multi-cluster v2 guide says plainly that without active health probing, "site failures are not detected automatically, and traffic continues to be routed to a failed site or split-brain partitions until manual intervention." The load balancer's health-check configuration is not an optional nice-to-have here — without it, "multi-region" doesn't actually buy you automatic failover.

    Case study: Riverside Health and HIPAA's Contingency Plan standard

    Chapter 11 covered the HIPAA Security Rule's technical safeguards — access control, audit controls, authentication. It didn't cover the Security Rule's administrative safeguards, and one of those, the Contingency Plan standard (45 CFR §164.308(a)(7)), is this chapter's direct territory. Confirmed from the regulation's own text, it requires covered entities to establish, as Required implementation specifications (not merely "addressable" — i.e., not optional with a documented justification to skip them):

    • a data backup plan — procedures to create and maintain retrievable exact copies of electronic PHI;
    • a disaster recovery plan — procedures to restore any loss of data; and
    • an emergency mode operation plan — procedures to enable continuation of critical business processes while operating in emergency mode.

    (Testing/revision procedures and an applications-and-data criticality analysis round out the standard as addressable specifications — still expected, but with more room for a documented, justified alternative.)

    Case study — Riverside Health

    Here's the part that's easy to miss: Riverside's patient portal's Keycloak realm is not just "an app that touches PHI" — it is one of the "critical business processes" the emergency mode operation plan has to keep running. If Keycloak is down during a regional outage, clinicians can't authenticate to reach patient records at the exact moment an emergency mode plan is supposed to be kicking in. That reframes this chapter's content as a direct HIPAA control, not a generic ops best practice: Riverside's data backup plan has to cover the identity layer's own database (not just clinical records), its disaster recovery plan has to include a tested Keycloak restore procedure, and if Riverside's facilities span more than one data center, its emergency mode plan is exactly the business justification that turns "maybe someday" multi-region Keycloak into a compliance-driven requirement. A realm-export cron job, on its own, satisfies none of the three — it has no restore-tested disaster-recovery story and nothing to say about emergency-mode continuity.

    Common mistakes / pitfalls

    • If a team calls kc.sh export their "backup," the cause is conflating a configuration-portability tool with a disaster-recovery mechanism — it skips events, persisted sessions, workflow state, and revoked tokens entirely.
    • If a restore "succeeds" but logins start failing with signature errors afterward, the cause is often realm signing keys not being part of what was restored or regenerated consistently — verify what your specific backup/restore path actually captures for keys rather than assuming.
    • If an import with --override corrupts session behavior afterward, the cause is almost always skipping the "stop every node first" step — the command doesn't coordinate with a running cache cluster, so the database and in-memory caches can disagree.
    • If someone claims "we're multi-region" because they deployed Keycloak in two data centers with an asynchronously-replicated database, the cause is skipping the one hard requirement every documented topology shares: synchronous cross-site replication. Async replication is a different (weaker) guarantee, not a shortcut to the same result.
    • If a "failover" never actually happens during a real site outage, the cause is usually missing or misconfigured load-balancer health probing — Keycloak's own docs warn that without it, traffic keeps hitting a dead or partitioned site until someone intervenes by hand.
    • If a compliance audit flags "no tested restore," the cause is almost always that a backup existed but nobody ever actually ran a restore from it end to end — a backup you haven't restored from is an untested assumption, not a control.

    Takeaways

    • The relational database is Keycloak's actual source of truth; back that up with your database vendor's own tooling, not Keycloak's export feature.
    • Realm export/import (kc.sh export/import) is for moving realm configuration between environments — a narrower job than disaster recovery, and Keycloak's own docs say so.
    • Keycloak's docs explicitly list what realm export/import does not capture: user/admin events, persisted sessions, workflow state, and revoked tokens.
    • Export and import both require the server to be stopped; importing with --override requires every node stopped, because the command doesn't coordinate with a live cache cluster.
    • The Admin Console's partial export/import is explicitly documented as unsuitable for backups or server-to-server transfers — it masks secrets and can't include users.
    • RPO measures data lost (backward from failure to last good backup); RTO measures downtime (forward from failure to restored service) — tightening either costs more engineering effort.
    • Keycloak documents three multi-site shapes: a single stretched cluster, multi-cluster v2 (current, database-coordinated), and multi-cluster v1 (deprecated, Infinispan-bridged) — all three require synchronous cross-site database replication.
    • Multi-region Keycloak guidance is honestly thinner than single-site HA guidance: no published failover runbook, no universal latency threshold, and an explicit instruction to validate your own RTO/RPO rather than trust a stated number.
    • Without active load-balancer health probing, a failed site is not automatically removed from rotation — Keycloak's docs name this directly as a split-brain-adjacent risk.
    • HIPAA's Contingency Plan standard (45 CFR §164.308(a)(7)) requires a data backup plan, a disaster recovery plan, and an emergency mode operation plan as Required specifications — and the identity layer itself is one of the "critical business processes" that standard protects, not just the clinical data behind it.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first.

    1. A colleague says "we don't need database backups, we already export every realm nightly." What's wrong with that plan, specifically?

      Model answerRealm export is a configuration-portability tool, not a backup mechanism — Keycloak's own docs confirm it skips user/admin events, persisted sessions, workflow state, and revoked tokens. A restore from that export would bring back realm and (optionally) user configuration, but with no audit history, no active sessions, and no record of anything mid-flight when it was taken. It also requires the server to be stopped to run reliably, which a "nightly automated export" plan may not even be accounting for against a live cluster.

    2. Distinguish RPO from RTO using a single concrete incident as the example.

      Model answerSay a database volume is corrupted at 2:00pm and the last good backup was taken at 2:00am. RPO is the 12-hour gap between that backup and the failure — the data (any role changes, new users, or sessions created between 2am and 2pm) that is permanently lost. RTO is the separate clock that starts at the moment of failure and ends when the service is usable again — if the restore-and-verify process takes until 4:00pm, RTO is two hours. They measure different things (data loss vs. downtime) and can be improved independently — more frequent backups shrink RPO; a faster/automated restore process shrinks RTO.

    3. Why does Keycloak's multi-cluster v2 topology require synchronous database replication between sites, rather than the simpler-to-operate asynchronous replication?

      Model answerBecause every Keycloak node at every site reads and writes the same logical database for critical state (sessions, realm data), and asynchronous replication means a write acknowledged at one site might not yet exist at the other if a failover happens immediately after. Keycloak's own docs warn that a database failover or stale read under this architecture "can result in data loss or an inconsistent state, including user sessions." Synchronous replication accepts a latency cost (the docs cite needing sub-10ms commit latency) in exchange for both sites always agreeing on the current state.

    4. A reader concludes "multi-cluster v2 gives Keycloak automatic, zero-downtime failover between regions." Is that an accurate reading of the live-verified documentation?

      Model answerNo. The documentation itself says there may be "downtime of several minutes" depending on database, load balancer, and Keycloak configuration, that node failure is typically detected within about a minute (not instantly), and — critically — that without active health probing, a failed site keeps receiving traffic or risks a split-brain partition "until manual intervention." It explicitly does not claim zero-downtime or fully automatic failover; it describes a topology that can tolerate a site failure, provided the operator has built and tested the health-probing and failover procedure around it.

    5. How does HIPAA's Contingency Plan standard change what "backing up Keycloak" means for Riverside Health, compared to a non-regulated organization running the same software?

      Model answerFor a non-regulated org, backup/DR planning is a good-engineering-practice decision made at the team's own discretion. For Riverside, 45 CFR §164.308(a)(7) makes a data backup plan, a disaster recovery plan, and an emergency mode operation plan Required implementation specifications under the HIPAA Security Rule — not optional, and auditable. It also reframes scope: because Keycloak is the authentication layer clinicians depend on to reach PHI, Keycloak's own availability during an emergency becomes part of what the emergency mode operation plan has to account for, not just the clinical database itself. A backup strategy that only covers patient records and ignores the identity system in front of them would leave that standard only partially satisfied.
