2. Identity Fundamentals

Every IAM system you will ever touch is, underneath, answering one question over and over: is this the entity it claims to be, and what do we actually know about it? This chapter gives you the vocabulary to answer that precisely — identity, identifier, credential, attribute, principal, and subject are not synonyms, and mixing them up is one of the most common ways beginners (and some very senior engineers) misdesign a system.

Why this matters

An IAM specialist spends a surprising amount of their career just getting this vocabulary straight with other people. A support ticket that says "the user's identity is wrong" could mean the identifier is mistyped, the credential was reset, an attribute is stale, or the account was never properly created in the first place — and the fix is different in every case. The generalized lifecycle you'll learn here (create → activate → modify → suspend → delete) is the shape every identity-bearing system implements, whether it's Keycloak, Active Directory, a homegrown users table, or a SaaS vendor's admin panel — learn the shape once, and you can navigate any of them.

Identity, identifier, credential, and attribute — four different things

Take a specific person: Jane Doe, an engineer at Northwind Apps. Beginners often use "identity" loosely to mean "the user," but a working IAM vocabulary needs four separate words for four separate things that are all true of Jane at once:

Four concepts, one person
ConceptDefinitionJane Doe's example
IdentityThe whole bundle — the record that ties an identifier, credentials, and attributes together as one entity inside a system.Jane's complete Northwind Apps employee record.
IdentifierThe unique value used to look the identity up within some namespace. It must be unique; it does not need to be secret. jane.doe@northwindapps.com
CredentialEvidence presented to prove control of the identifier. Credentials are the thing that must stay secret, and the thing you rotate. Her password, plus a one-time code from an authenticator app.
AttributeA fact recorded about the identity. Attributes describe; they don't prove anything on their own.department: Engineering
Pitfall

Changing an attribute is not the same event as changing a credential, and systems that treat them the same cause real incidents — e.g. forcing a password reset because someone's job title changed, or silently invalidating a session because an unrelated profile field was edited.

Case study — Northwind Apps

At Northwind Apps, the attribute tenantId: acme-corp on a customer-company employee's identity is what lets the application show that user only Acme Corp's project boards — not Globex's. Change that one attribute and her visible access changes instantly, without touching her credential or her identifier at all. That's the practical payoff of keeping these four concepts separate: you can reason about exactly which one changed and what effect that should have.

Principal vs. subject

These two terms get used interchangeably in casual conversation, and most of the time that's harmless. But they have a formal distinction that matters once tokens and sessions enter the picture:

Key idea

A principal is the entity being identified — the real-world person, service, or device an identity refers to. It exists independent of any login event. A subject is that principal as represented inside one active security context, such as a signed token or a live session. The same principal produces a brand-new subject every time it authenticates.

Case study — Meridian Trust

If a Meridian Trust customer's mobile-banking access token is stolen and used by an attacker before it expires, the attacker has gained control of a valid subject without being the principal it was issued to. That distinction is exactly why token theft is treated as a serious incident even though the customer's actual identity — their principal — was never touched: the thing that was compromised was one active security context, not the person.

The identity lifecycle, generalized

Regardless of which product implements it, every identity moves through the same generalized stages over its life: create it, activate it so it can actually be used, modify it as facts about it change, suspend it when access needs to stop temporarily, and eventually delete it. An identity can arrive at "create" through a few different doors — a human administrator creating it directly (manual creation), the principal filling out a signup form themselves (self-registration), or the identity being created automatically from an external identity provider's data the first time someone logs in (federated/brokered creation — full depth in chapter 7). Where it comes from doesn't change what happens to it afterward.

Case study — Meridian Trust

When Meridian Trust's fraud team flags unusual activity on a teller's account, the response is to suspend it, not delete it — login is blocked while HR investigates, but the identity record and its history stay intact. If HR clears her a week later, the account is reactivated rather than recreated from scratch. Meridian's auditors specifically care about that distinction: suspend-then-reinstate leaves one continuous, explainable record; delete-then-recreate breaks that continuity and can obscure that it was the same person the whole time.

In Keycloak

Chapter 22 puts hands on exactly this: Keycloak's Users section stores the identifier/credential/ attribute split you just learned — a username/email identifier, a Credentials tab for passwords and OTP, and a free-form Attributes tab — plus an Enabled/Disabled toggle that implements the generalized "suspend" stage of this lifecycle.

Human and non-human identities

Not every identity in a system represents a person. A human identity represents an employee, customer, patient, or clinician. A non-human identity represents something acting on an organization's behalf without a person sitting behind it in real time: a service account an application uses to authenticate, a machine identity tied to a device or server (often backed by a certificate), or a workload identity assigned to a running piece of software — a container, a function — so it can authenticate without a long-lived secret baked into its code. This distinction matters because non-human identities are often long-lived, broadly privileged, and unattended, which means they need their own review and least-privilege discipline rather than being lumped in with human accounts and forgotten.

Case study — Riverside Health

Riverside Health's lab-results system doesn't only have doctor and nurse identities — it also runs a service account that polls the hospital's imaging system every five minutes and pushes new scans into the records system. That account never logs in through a browser, never forgets a password, and shouldn't be granted the same broad access a clinician has. In more real incidents than you'd expect, exactly that over-privileging is what goes wrong.

Meet the three organizations you'll see for the rest of this course

Starting here, every scenario, lab, and case study in this course uses one of the same three organizations, so your mental model compounds instead of resetting with every new example.

Northwind Apps is a B2B SaaS company that sells project-management software to other businesses. Its identity population splits cleanly into two groups: internal staff (engineers, support reps, sales) who need workforce access to Northwind's own systems, and the employees of its business customers — thousands of people across hundreds of customer companies — who need to log into the Northwind product itself. Because each customer company is effectively its own tenant, Northwind cares enormously about CIAM (customer identity and access management) at scale and about enterprise SSO: a customer's IT admin wants their own employees signing in with their own corporate identity provider, not a Northwind-issued password. Northwind will be our running example for multi-tenant SaaS identity and enterprise federation.

Riverside Health is a regional hospital network. Its identity population is split between clinician workforce identities — doctors, nurses, and administrative staff who need fast, reliable access to clinical systems, often from shared workstations, where authentication friction can cost seconds that matter — and patient identities, who use a patient portal to view records, message their care team, and pay bills. Riverside operates under HIPAA, so every one of those identities, human or not, carries obligations: strong authentication, tight audit trails, and access that maps to a legitimate clinical need. Riverside will be our running example for regulated workforce IAM and patient-facing CIAM existing side by side in one organization.

Meridian Trust is a retail bank. Its identity population includes bank employees — tellers, loan officers, back-office staff, each with very different authorization needs — and retail customers who bank online and through a mobile app. Because Meridian is a financial institution under PCI-DSS and heavy fraud pressure, its identity program has to support step-up authentication (asking for a stronger credential only when risk is high, such as before a wire transfer) and fraud-aware authentication that treats a login attempt differently depending on device, location, and behavior. Meridian will be our running example for high-assurance consumer authentication and risk-based access decisions.

Where governance picks this up

Creating and deleting identities — the first and last stages of the lifecycle you just saw — also have a formal governance side: approvals, workflows, and the joiner-mover-leaver process. That's provisioning and deprovisioning, covered briefly here and in full depth in chapter 10.

Common mistakes

  • If you see an application forcing a password reset whenever someone's job title changes, the cause is usually treating an attribute change as if it were a credential change — the two are unrelated events.
  • If you see an audit log that can't say whether an account was deleted or merely disabled, the cause is usually a system that never separated "suspend" from "delete" as distinct lifecycle stages.
  • If you see a service account holding the same broad permissions as a senior administrator and nobody can explain why, the cause is usually that it was provisioned once, years ago, and never re-reviewed the way a human identity would be.
  • If you see two engineers arguing about whether "the user" changed after a token refresh, the cause is usually that they're using "principal" and "subject" interchangeably when the distinction actually matters for the argument they're having.

Glossary terms introduced here

Takeaways

  • Identity, identifier, credential, and attribute are four different concepts describing the same real-world entity — don't conflate them.
  • An identifier only needs to be unique, not secret; a credential is the thing that must stay secret.
  • Attributes describe an identity; they don't prove anything about it the way a credential does.
  • A principal is the real-world entity; a subject is that principal as represented in one active security context — a single principal can produce many subjects over time.
  • The identity lifecycle generalizes to create → activate → modify → suspend → delete, regardless of which product implements it.
  • An identity can enter the "create" stage through manual creation, self-registration, or federated/brokered creation — same destination, different doors.
  • Suspension and deletion are not the same thing, and audit trails care about the difference.
  • Not every identity is human — service accounts, machine identities, and workload identities need their own review and least-privilege discipline.
  • Northwind Apps, Riverside Health, and Meridian Trust each manage a different mix of human identity types and will recur for the rest of this course.
  • These vendor-agnostic lifecycle stages come back concretely once Keycloak is installed, in chapter 22.
