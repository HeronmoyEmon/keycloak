7. SSO & Federation Concepts

Chapter 1 mentioned single sign-on, identity brokering, and federation in passing, as things a centralized IAM system makes possible. This chapter gives each of those a precise, formal definition — the roles involved, the mechanics, and the two different ways an external identity source can be plugged in — because chapters 15, 17, 31, and 32 will all assume you already have this vocabulary exactly right, not just a casual feel for it.

Why this matters

An IAM specialist is constantly asked questions that hinge on getting these words precise: "can our customers use their own login?" (brokering, or federation, depending on what they actually have), "why did the user get created automatically — we never added them?" (JIT provisioning), "why doesn't SSO work across these two apps?" (a trust or session-sharing problem). Confusing brokering with federation, or SP with RP, doesn't just sound sloppy in a design review — it leads to building the wrong integration, because the two patterns involve genuinely different protocols, different data flows, and different failure modes.

Recall from chapter 1

Chapter 1 defined SSO as "one login session usable across multiple connected applications," identity brokering as an intermediary that authenticates a user via an external IdP and translates the result for connecting apps, and federation as connecting to an existing external identity store so its accounts can be reused. All three were deliberately brief there. This chapter is where each one gets its full mechanics, its own worked example, and the formal role vocabulary (IdP/SP/RP) that the rest of the course builds on.

The formal roles: Identity Provider, Service Provider, Relying Party

Every SSO, brokering, or federation scenario involves the same two roles, no matter which protocol implements it:

  • An Identity Provider (IdP) — the system that holds credentials, performs authentication, and asserts "this is who the user is" to someone else. This term is shared across protocols and is safe to use regardless of which one you're discussing.
  • The system that relies on the IdP's assertion, instead of authenticating the user itself. This second role has two different names in the two protocol families you'll study in Part III: SAML calls it the Service Provider (SP); OpenID Connect calls it the Relying Party (RP).
Pitfall — a very common beginner confusion

SP and RP are not two different roles — they are the same role, named differently by two different protocol vocabularies. A SAML-speaking application is "the SP"; an OIDC-speaking application is "the RP"; both are just "the application that trusts an external IdP instead of authenticating users itself." If you ever catch yourself asking "is this app an SP or an RP?" as if the answer reveals something about its architecture, stop — the real question is "which protocol is this app speaking," and the role-name just follows from that. Chapter 15 (OIDC) will use RP throughout; chapter 17 (SAML) will use SP throughout; neither chapter re-explains this overlap — it's established here.

SSO revisited: the mechanics, conceptually

Chapter 1 defined SSO as one login session usable across multiple connected applications. Here's how that actually works, at a conceptual level — without committing to a specific protocol yet, since that's Part III's job (chapter 13 for OAuth, chapter 15 for OIDC, chapter 17 for SAML).

A user visits App A and is sent to a shared IdP to log in. Rather than keeping that login result local to App A, the IdP establishes its own session, tracked by something the user's browser carries from that point on (conceptually, a cookie scoped to the IdP). When the same user then visits App B — a different application, also configured to trust that same IdP — App B redirects the browser to the IdP exactly the way App A did. Because the browser already carries the IdP's session, the IdP recognizes the user immediately, without asking for credentials again, and sends back a fresh assertion for App B. From the user's point of view, they only logged in once; from the system's point of view, the IdP's session was recognized a second time by a second redirect.

Key idea

SSO is not "the same password reused everywhere" — that's a weaker, insecure imitation. Real SSO means the authentication act happens once, against one IdP, and every connected application's trust in the result is established through a redirect-and-assertion handshake with that same IdP, not through the user re-typing credentials app by app.

In Keycloak

A Keycloak realm is the shared IdP in this picture: every client application registered in that realm redirects to the same realm login endpoint and shares the browser SSO session Keycloak maintains. The session mechanics — cookies, SSO session timeouts, and how logout propagates across clients — get full hands-on depth in chapter 29.

Trust between IdP and SP/RP

None of the SSO handshake in Figure 7.2 works unless the IdP and the relying application already agree, in advance, on a few things: that they recognize each other at all, how to verify that an assertion genuinely came from the claimed IdP, and where the browser is allowed to be sent back to afterward. Collectively this is called establishing trust, and in practice it has three ingredients:

An IdP doesn't automatically trust every application that redirects to it, and an application doesn't automatically trust every assertion that comes back; both sides register with each other first.

Brokering vs. federation, in full depth

Chapter 1 introduced these as two different things that both involve an external identity source. Here's the distinction made precise, with one worked example each that you should not be able to confuse afterward.

Key idea

Brokering delegates the act of authentication to an external IdP that the user already has an account with — the broker doesn't store or manage that external account at all, it just relays the result. Federation connects to an external identity store or directory that already holds a population of accounts, so that existing population can be reused by the IAM system without copying every record into it. Brokering is about delegating one login event; federation is about reusing an entire existing directory of accounts.

Case study — Northwind Apps

Northwind's enterprise customers increasingly ask: "can our employees log into Northwind with their own corporate identity, instead of a Northwind-issued password?" In this relationship, Northwind is the SP/RP — it's the application relying on someone else's authentication — and the customer's own corporate IdP is the external IdP being trusted. This is brokering, not federation: Northwind's Keycloak realm never touches the customer's directory directly; it configures a trust relationship with the customer's IdP and lets that IdP do the authenticating, exactly the shape chapter 31 implements.

Case study — Riverside Health

Riverside Health's clinicians already have accounts in the hospital's Active Directory — IT provisions and deprovisions them there as part of onboarding and offboarding. Rather than maintaining a second, separate set of Keycloak-local credentials that could drift out of sync, Riverside's Keycloak realm is configured for user federation against that AD: a clinician's login is checked against the existing directory itself. There is no second password to remember, and no second place for IT to update when someone leaves. This is the federation pattern, not brokering — Riverside's Keycloak isn't delegating to an external login page, it's reaching into an existing store of accounts it doesn't own.

JIT (just-in-time) provisioning

Chapter 2 named "federated/brokered creation" as one of the three doors into the identity lifecycle's create stage, alongside manual creation and self-registration, and deferred the detail to this chapter. Here it is: just-in-time (JIT) provisioning is what happens at that door. The first time a user successfully authenticates through a broker or a federated directory, the local IAM system creates a matching local identity automatically, populated from whatever attributes the external IdP or directory handed back (name, email, group memberships) — instead of an administrator having to pre-create every account by hand before anyone can log in.

This is still the same generalized lifecycle from chapter 2 — create, activate, modify, suspend, delete — JIT provisioning only changes how the create step gets triggered and where the initial attribute values come from. Everything downstream (suspending the account, auditing its access, eventually deleting it) works exactly as it would for a manually created or self-registered identity.

Pitfall

JIT provisioning means the first login an external user ever performs is also, silently, an account-creation event. If nobody realizes that, "why did a local account for this person appear without anyone creating it?" turns into a confusing support ticket instead of an expected behavior. It's also why brokered/federated logins deserve the same lifecycle discipline as any other account going forward — a JIT-provisioned identity still needs to be reviewed, suspended, or deleted when it should be, it just arrived through a different door.

Common mistakes

  • If you see someone ask "is this app an SP or an RP?" as though the answer changes the architecture, the cause is treating SP/RP as two different roles instead of the same role under two protocols' vocabularies.
  • If you see "SSO" used to mean "the same password typed into every app," the cause is confusing credential reuse with a real shared-session handshake against one IdP.
  • If you see a team build a federation connector when the actual requirement was "let users log in with their existing Google/Microsoft account," the cause is reaching for the wrong pattern — that request is brokering (delegating one login) not federation (reusing a directory).
  • If you see a brokered or federated user's local account appear with no obvious creation event in the admin trail, the cause is usually JIT provisioning working as designed, not a bug — check the federation/broker logs, not the manual-creation audit path.
  • If you see an integration fail with a vague "untrusted" or signature error, the cause is almost always a trust-configuration gap — mismatched metadata, an expired certificate, or a redirect URI that was never registered — not a bug in the protocol itself.

Glossary terms introduced here

Takeaways

  • Identity Provider (IdP) is a shared term across protocols; the role that relies on the IdP's assertion is called Service Provider (SP) in SAML and Relying Party (RP) in OIDC — same role, different vocabulary.
  • Real SSO means the authentication act happens once against a shared IdP, and each connected application recognizes that result via its own redirect-and-assertion handshake — not shared passwords.
  • SSO works because the browser carries a session the IdP recognizes on each subsequent redirect, not because any one application remembers the user.
  • Trust between an IdP and an SP/RP must be configured in advance — metadata, certificates/signing keys, and registered redirect URIs — never assumed.
  • Brokering delegates the act of authentication to an external IdP the user already has an account with (e.g. "Login with Google"); the broker never stores that external credential.
  • Federation connects to an existing external identity store or directory (e.g. LDAP/AD) so its accounts can be reused, rather than delegating a single login event.
  • JIT provisioning is the mechanism behind the "federated/brokered creation" door from chapter 2's identity lifecycle: a local identity is created automatically from external IdP/directory data on first login.
  • A JIT-provisioned identity still goes through the full identity lifecycle afterward — it just entered through a different door than manual creation or self-registration.
  • Northwind Apps is the SP/RP trusting its enterprise customers' corporate IdPs (brokering); Riverside Health reuses its hospital AD directly for clinician logins (federation) — these are not the same pattern, even though both involve an "external" identity source.
