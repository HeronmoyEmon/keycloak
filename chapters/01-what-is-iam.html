    1. What Is IAM?

    Every piece of software that has users eventually has to answer two questions: "who is this?" and "what are they allowed to do?" Identity and Access Management is the discipline — and the category of software — built around answering those two questions once, correctly, and reusing the answer everywhere instead of letting every application invent its own version.

    Why this matters

    An IAM specialist's job starts right here, before any protocol or product. If you can't explain to a skeptical engineering lead why a company should centralize authentication instead of letting each team roll its own, you can't justify the budget, the migration pain, or the extra deployment a central identity system adds. This chapter builds that argument from first principles, vendor-agnostic, so that when Keycloak shows up starting in Part IV it reads as "an implementation of this idea" rather than "a tool I was told to learn."

    The business problem: ten applications, ten logins

    Picture a mid-size company with ten internal and customer-facing applications: an HR portal, a support ticketing tool, a customer-facing dashboard, an internal wiki, a billing system, and so on. Nobody plans to build authentication ten times — but by default, that's exactly what happens. Each application's team, on their own schedule, ends up writing a login form, a password column in a database, a "forgot password" email flow, account lockout logic, and — if the product grows enough to need it — a "Sign in with Google" button and some kind of multi-factor authentication. Each of those ten implementations is done by a different team, at a different level of care, on a different timeline, and reviewed by whoever happened to be free that sprint.

    The predictable result is not ten solid login systems; it's ten slightly different login systems, each with its own bugs, its own password-hashing choices (some stronger than others), its own definition of "locked out," and its own audit logging — if it logs anything at all. A user who needs access to three of those ten apps has three separate passwords, three separate account lifecycles, and three separate places a credential can leak from. The company has no single answer to "who can currently log into anything," because that answer is scattered across ten databases that don't talk to each other.

    Key idea

    IAM is not primarily a security feature — it's a de-duplication strategy. Authentication and authorization are the same hard problem no matter which application asks the question, so IAM solves it once, well, and lets every application consume the answer instead of re-deriving it.

    Authentication and authorization, in one sentence each

    Two words get used constantly in this space, and this chapter only needs you to be able to tell them apart, not master them — chapter 3 covers the full "AAA" model (authentication, authorization, and accounting) in depth, including why conflating the two causes real security bugs.

    • Authentication (AuthN) answers "who are you?" — proving an identity, typically with a password, a one-time code, a hardware key, or some combination of those.
    • Authorization (AuthZ) answers "what are you allowed to do, now that I know who you are?" — deciding whether a now-known identity can read a file, approve an invoice, or view a patient record.

    A system can authenticate someone perfectly and still make the wrong authorization decision (or vice versa) — they are genuinely separate problems that happen to be solved by the same class of software, which is part of why they get bundled into the acronym IAM rather than being two unrelated fields.

    Why centralizing matters

    Generalizing the ten-apps scenario above, here is the same shape of problem stated as a table — each row is something that goes wrong (or goes un-done) when authentication is left to individual application teams, and what a dedicated IAM system solves by existing as a shared dependency instead.

    Problems that appear when every application handles its own identity, and what a centralized IAM system solves
    Problem without centralized IAMWhat a general-purpose IAM system solves
    Every app builds its own login form and password storage, at wildly different quality levelsOne vetted, maintained authentication component every app delegates to
    Users juggle a different password per app, and reuse them out of fatigueSingle Sign-On: one login session usable across every connected application
    Getting authentication security details right (hashing, rate limits, session handling) is easy to get subtly wrongBattle-tested, widely reviewed security logic used by many organizations, not reinvented per app
    "Login with Google/Microsoft" needs its own integration work in every app that wants itSocial and enterprise login handled once, centrally, and offered to every connected app
    Enterprise customers expect to log in with their own corporate directory, which each app can't realistically support aloneBuilt-in federation to external directories and identity providers
    An auditor asks "who could access this system last quarter" and nobody has one answerA single, consistent audit trail of authentication and access events across apps
    Offboarding a departing employee means chasing down access in ten separate systemsOne place to disable an identity and have that change apply everywhere it's connected
    Mobile and API clients need a secure, standard way to carry proof of login between servicesStandardized, interoperable tokens (the subject of Part III) instead of ad-hoc session cookies per app

    What an IAM system typically provides

    Not every IAM product implements every one of these, and not every organization needs every one of these — but this is the usual feature set the category has converged on. Each line below points to where this course covers that piece in depth.

    • Single Sign-On (SSO) — one authenticated session usable across multiple applications without logging in again for each one. Full mechanics in chapter 7; how Keycloak implements SSO sessions is chapter 29.
    • Identity brokering — letting users authenticate via an external provider (Google, a corporate SAML IdP) while the IAM system "brokers" the result into something your applications understand. Chapter 7 introduces the concept; chapter 31 implements it in Keycloak.
    • Federation — connecting to an existing identity store (like a company's LDAP or Active Directory) instead of migrating every account into the IAM system. Chapter 4 covers directory concepts; chapter 32 federates Keycloak with a real directory.
    • Role-based and fine-grained authorization — deciding what an authenticated identity can do, from simple role labels up to attribute- and relationship-based policies. Chapter 6 covers the models; chapters 23 and 37 implement them in Keycloak.
    • Multi-factor authentication (MFA) — requiring more than a password: a one-time code, a hardware key, biometrics. Chapter 5 covers factors and risk-based auth in depth.
    • Self-service account management — letting users reset their own password, update their profile, or review their own active sessions without filing a help-desk ticket.
    • Admin tooling — a console (and, usually, an API) for administrators to manage users, applications, and policy without touching a database directly.
    • Extensibility — a plugin or SPI-style architecture so an organization can add custom authentication steps, custom data sources, or custom branding without forking the product. Keycloak's plugin architecture is the subject of Part VIII.
    In Keycloak

    Every item in that list maps to a concrete screen in Keycloak's admin console once it's installed: SSO and sessions live under a realm's Sessions view (chapter 29), MFA setup lives under Authentication → Required Actions (chapter 30), and self-service lives in the separate per-realm Account Console at /realms/<realm>/account (chapter 22). None of that is installed yet — chapter 20 is where you first stand up a running Keycloak instance.

    A quick illustration: one identity, many applications

    Suppose a mid-size organization has ten internal tools and one employee, Priya, who needs access to three of them: the HR portal, the wiki, and a reporting dashboard. Without a shared identity layer, "Priya" is really three unrelated database rows that happen to share a name — three passwords, three profile records, and three places her access has to be revoked separately the day she leaves. With a centralized identity layer, "Priya" is one identity that the HR portal, the wiki, and the dashboard each independently trust; disabling that one identity on her last day removes her access everywhere at once, and a single login screen gets her into all three without re-entering credentials. This course will use three recurring example organizations — introduced formally in the next chapter — to make scenarios like this concrete and consistent instead of inventing a new one-off company for every example.

    Pros and cons of adopting an IAM system

    Centralizing identity is a real engineering and organizational investment, not a free win. Weigh both sides before recommending it.

    Trade-offs of adopting a dedicated IAM system
    ProsCons
    Greatly reduces the chance of building authentication wrong (weak hashing, no rate limiting, no lockout)Learning curve: a new system every team must understand, at least at the integration level
    One login, one password, one MFA setup for the user across every connected applicationOperational overhead: the IAM system itself needs to be deployed, patched, monitored, and kept available
    Centralized, consistent audit trail — one place to answer "who accessed what, when"A new single point of failure: if the IAM system is down, every connected app's login is down too
    Offboarding/onboarding becomes a single action instead of N separate onesMigration cost: existing applications with their own login systems have to be migrated, not just new ones
    Standard protocols (Part III) mean new applications integrate in days, not monthsRequires genuine protocol literacy (OAuth/OIDC/SAML) on every integrating team, which has its own learning curve
    Features like social login, federation, and fine-grained authorization are built once and reusedOver-centralization risk: policy decisions that should live in individual apps can get awkwardly forced into the IAM layer
    Pitfall

    Treating "we adopted an IAM system" as equivalent to "we are now secure" is a common mistake. The IAM system removes a large, repeated source of bugs, but it introduces its own configuration surface — a misconfigured redirect URI or an overly permissive role mapping is just as exploitable as a home-grown login bug used to be. Centralizing risk doesn't eliminate it; it concentrates it somewhere worth securing carefully (chapters 9 and 18 cover exactly this).

    When to use one — and when it's overkill

    A dedicated IAM system is not the right call for every situation. Generalizing: the more applications share users, the more those users need to cross application boundaries in a single session, and the more compliance or audit pressure exists, the stronger the case for centralizing. The case weakens fast as those factors disappear.

    • Reach for a centralized IAM system when: you have multiple applications that share a user base; users need SSO across them; you need social login or federation with an external directory; you're under compliance pressure that demands audit trails and strong authentication; or you simply don't want to keep re-solving authentication from scratch.
    • It's probably overkill when: you have a single, small internal tool with one or a handful of trusted admin users and no plan to add more applications; you're prototyping something disposable; or you're building for an extremely resource-constrained environment where running even a lightweight identity server is disproportionate to the problem. In those cases, a simple built-in login or a managed cloud provider's lightest-weight auth offering is often the more sensible choice — and that's a legitimate engineering decision, not a shortcut to feel guilty about.

    Where Keycloak fits

    This course needs a concrete, hands-on vehicle to turn every concept above into something you can actually configure and break, and it uses Keycloak, Red Hat's open-source IAM project, for that job starting in Part IV. Keycloak is one of several mature products that implement the ideas in this chapter — commercial alternatives like Okta, Auth0, Microsoft Entra ID, and Ping Identity solve largely the same problems with different trade-offs around hosting, pricing, and ecosystem fit. Chapter 12 is dedicated to that wider market comparison; nothing here is a pitch for Keycloak over its competitors — it's simply the one this course picks so you have one real system to build against instead of only reading about the category in the abstract.

    Takeaways

    • IAM exists to solve "who is this, and what can they do" once instead of letting every application solve it separately and inconsistently.
    • Without centralization, applications don't fail to build authentication — they build ten different, uneven versions of it.
    • Authentication (who are you) and authorization (what can you do) are distinct problems that happen to be solved by the same category of system.
    • Centralizing identity turns offboarding, auditing, and policy changes into one action instead of N separate ones.
    • SSO, identity brokering, federation, fine-grained authorization, MFA, self-service, admin tooling, and extensibility are the common feature set of an IAM product, not a Keycloak-specific list.
    • Adopting an IAM system is a real trade-off: it removes a recurring source of security bugs but adds operational overhead and a new single point of failure.
    • Centralizing authentication is not the same as being secure — it relocates risk to a configuration surface that must itself be secured carefully.
    • A single small internal tool with a handful of trusted users is a legitimate case where full IAM adoption is overkill.
    • Keycloak is this course's chosen implementation of these ideas, not the only one — chapter 12 covers the broader market.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first — the point is to check your own reasoning against it, not to read the answer first.

    1. Without naming any specific product, explain to a colleague in two sentences why ten applications each writing their own login code is a worse outcome than it sounds, even if each one "works."

      Model answerEach implementation ends up with its own subtly different password-hashing strength, lockout policy, and audit logging — "works" individually doesn't mean "works consistently," and an attacker only needs to find the weakest of the ten. There's also no single place to answer "who can log into anything," so something as routine as offboarding one employee turns into ten separate manual steps instead of one.

    2. Give an example of a decision that is authentication, and a separate example of a decision that is authorization, from an app you use regularly.

      Model answerAuthentication: your banking app confirming your fingerprint matches the account holder before letting you in at all. Authorization: once you're in, the app deciding whether you can view a specific account's statement or only your own — a separate check made after authentication has already succeeded, and one authentication alone can't answer.

    3. Pick one row from the "why centralizing matters" table and argue the opposite side: under what circumstances would the "problem" column actually be an acceptable trade-off?

      Model answerTake "every app builds its own login form." For a two-person internal prototype that will be thrown away in a month, writing a basic login form directly into the app is a reasonable trade-off — the integration overhead of standing up a full IAM system would cost more engineering time than it saves, and there's no audit or compliance pressure yet to justify it. The "problem" only becomes a real problem once more apps, more users, or real compliance stakes show up.

    4. Name one scenario where adopting a full IAM system would be clearly overkill, and explain what you'd use instead.

      Model answerA single internal admin dashboard used by three trusted engineers at a small startup, with no plan to add more users or applications. A simple built-in login check is proportionate here; operating a dedicated IAM system for three people adds real ongoing overhead (patching, monitoring, availability) with no corresponding benefit, since there's no second application to share that identity with yet.

    5. If an organization centralizes authentication but then misconfigures it, what is lost compared to the "ten separate apps" world — is the organization actually worse off, or just differently exposed?

      Model answerDifferently exposed, not automatically worse. In the ten-apps world, a bug in one app's login only exposes that app. A misconfigured central IAM system — an overly permissive redirect URI, say — can expose every connected application at once, because the risk is now concentrated in one place instead of spread across ten. That concentration is exactly why securing the IAM layer deserves at least as much care as securing the ten separate apps used to need (chapters 9 and 18 cover this directly).
