23. Roles & Groups

Chapter 22 gave northwind-apps two real people: jane.doe and priya.patel. Real people with accounts but no access are just names in a database — this chapter is where they get something to do. You'll create three realm roles and two groups, assign Jane as a tenant administrator, assign Priya as a support agent, and then do the one thing this course has been building toward since chapter 10: literally move Priya from Support to Platform Engineering in a real running realm, and prove — with real kcadm.sh output, not a description — that her old access was actually removed, not just buried under new access she also now has.

Why this matters

Every access-control system an IAM specialist will ever operate needs some structure between "the system has thousands of users" and "every permission is checked one user at a time." Roles and groups are that structure in Keycloak, and they're also where the single most common governance mistake in the entire field actually happens — not in a policy document, but in the two or three admin-console clicks (or kcadm.sh commands) someone makes when a person changes teams. Knowing the mechanics cold, and knowing exactly which step gets skipped when someone gets it wrong, is what this chapter is for.

What concept is this?

Chapter 6 named five authorization models and said Keycloak's realm roles and client roles, "assigned to users directly or via groups, are a direct RBAC implementation" — and promised that this chapter would cover "creating roles, composite roles, and group-based role assignment hands-on against a running realm." That promise is this chapter. Chapter 10 then taught the Joiner-Mover-Leaver vocabulary and specifically warned that a Mover event is "not purely additive" — the most common beginner mistake in identity governance is granting new access without removing the old. Chapter 10's own worked example was Priya, moving from Northwind's support team to its platform engineering team. That was a conceptual walkthrough with no real system behind it. This chapter reproduces it, by name, in a real Keycloak realm, with real captured command output proving the old role was actually gone afterward.

Realm roles vs. client roles

Keycloak has two kinds of roles, distinguished by scope. A realm role lives in the realm's own namespace and is visible to — and can be checked by — every client registered in that realm. A client role lives inside one specific client's namespace; Keycloak's own documentation puts it plainly: "Clients can define roles that are specific to them. This is basically a role namespace dedicated to the client." The same role name can exist once as a realm role and separately, meaning something entirely different, as a role on two different clients — there's no collision, because each lives in its own namespace.

This chapter only creates realm roles, and that's a deliberate scope choice, not an oversight: tenant-admin, support-agent, and platform-engineer describe Jane's and Priya's organizational roles at Northwind Apps — true regardless of which specific application they're using at the moment. A realm role is the right tool for "what kind of employee is this," checked the same way by every client in the realm. Client roles are the right tool for "what can this identity do inside this one application" — chapter 24 creates the northwind-dashboard client and gives it its own client-role namespace, so hold that thought until then.

Composite roles: roles that contain other roles

Keycloak's documentation defines a composite role plainly: "A composite role is a role that can be associated with other roles." Map a user to a composite role, and Keycloak grants every role it's associated with too — the composite role doesn't carry its own separate set of permissions so much as it bundles existing roles under one name. In the Admin Console, this is done from a role's own page: open Realm Roles, click the role, and from its Action list choose Add associated roles to pick which other realm-level or client-level roles it should carry.

This lab doesn't build a composite role — tenant-admin, support-agent, and platform-engineer are kept as three independent, non-overlapping roles on purpose, because the Mover payoff later in this chapter depends on them being independent (if platform-engineer quietly included support-agent as an associated role, moving Priya wouldn't actually remove her support access — it would just hide it one level deeper). But composite roles are a real, commonly used mechanism worth knowing exists: a Northwind tenant-admin role, for example, could reasonably be built as a composite of a narrower user-manager role and a narrower settings-editor role, so each piece stays independently assignable while tenant-admin still grants the full bundle in one assignment.

Groups: carrying access for a set of people at once

A group is a named collection of users. Keycloak's docs state the mechanism exactly: "Users that become members of a group inherit the attributes and role mappings that group defines." A group can carry its own role mappings (and attributes), and every current member inherits them automatically — add someone to the group and they immediately get everything the group carries; remove them and they immediately lose it. Groups can also nest: creating a new group under an existing parent makes it a subgroup, which inherits the parent's role mappings in addition to its own.

Groups and roles solve a related but distinct problem. A role answers "what can this identity do." A group answers "who does this identity sit with organizationally" — and, because Keycloak lets a group carry role mappings, a group becomes a convenient way to grant a whole team's worth of people the same role(s) in one place, instead of assigning (and later individually revoking) the same role on every member one at a time. This chapter's Engineering and Support groups aren't strictly necessary to reproduce chapter 10's Mover scenario — the role assignment alone would do that — but a real organization almost always tracks team membership as groups anyway, and the Mover payoff is more honest if it moves Priya's full organizational picture, group membership included, not just one role.

Roles vs. groups: what each one actually answers
QuestionAnswered by
"What is this identity allowed to do?"Roles (realm or client), whether assigned directly or inherited
"Which team/org unit does this identity belong to?"Groups
"If I add someone to this team, what do they automatically get?"The group's own role mappings, inherited by every member
"Does a role grant other roles automatically?"Composite roles — the role mechanism's own form of bundling, independent of groups

The Mover payoff: reproducing chapter 10, for real

Here is the scenario chapter 10 described conceptually, run for real. Northwind Apps moves Priya from the support team onto the platform engineering team. Chapter 10's pitfall callout named the trap exactly: "the naive implementation just grants the new team's access on top of whatever the person already had." The fix isn't exotic — it's two kinds of grant and two kinds of removal, done together, as one event:

  1. Grant the new access — add Priya to Engineering and assign her platform-engineer:

    docker compose exec keycloak /opt/keycloak/bin/kcadm.sh update -r northwind-apps \
  "users/758fcf4b-057a-41ca-a8d0-420ccc8f501b/groups/a18c8082-db97-44e2-9ccd-f2d32e2ce256" \
  -s realm=northwind-apps \
  -s userId=758fcf4b-057a-41ca-a8d0-420ccc8f501b \
  -s groupId=a18c8082-db97-44e2-9ccd-f2d32e2ce256 -n

docker compose exec keycloak /opt/keycloak/bin/kcadm.sh add-roles -r northwind-apps \
  --uusername priya.patel --rolename platform-engineer

    If the Mover event stopped here — which is exactly the mistake chapter 10 warned about — Priya would now hold both teams' access at once. Nothing above removed anything yet.

  2. Remove the old access — the step chapter 10 called the one beginners skip:

    docker compose exec keycloak /opt/keycloak/bin/kcadm.sh delete -r northwind-apps \
  "users/758fcf4b-057a-41ca-a8d0-420ccc8f501b/groups/c1a1f336-455f-40dc-9793-af85a4e0dbd3"

docker compose exec keycloak /opt/keycloak/bin/kcadm.sh remove-roles -r northwind-apps \
  --uusername priya.patel --rolename support-agent

    Two commands, each the exact mirror of a command two steps earlier in this chapter: the same group-membership endpoint, called with delete instead of update; the same role-assignment mechanism, called with remove-roles instead of add-roles. Keycloak doesn't do anything automatically here — removing the old access is a decision a human (or a script) has to make and execute, exactly as deliberately as granting the new access was.

  3. Confirm the after-state independently — the same two read-only commands run before the move, run again:

    docker compose exec keycloak /opt/keycloak/bin/kcadm.sh get \
  "users/758fcf4b-057a-41ca-a8d0-420ccc8f501b/role-mappings/realm" -r northwind-apps --fields name

docker compose exec keycloak /opt/keycloak/bin/kcadm.sh get \
  "users/758fcf4b-057a-41ca-a8d0-420ccc8f501b/groups" -r northwind-apps --fields name
    [ {
  "name" : "platform-engineer"
}, {
  "name" : "default-roles-northwind-apps"
} ]
[ {
  "name" : "Engineering"
} ]
Case study — the before/after, side by side

Northwind Apps's Priya, before the move: realm roles support-agent + default-roles-northwind-apps; groups: Support. After the move: realm roles platform-engineer + default-roles-northwind-apps; groups: Engineering. support-agent and Support do not appear anywhere in the after-state — not demoted, not hidden, not inherited from somewhere else. They are gone, confirmed by a get call that queries Keycloak's own current state, not by trusting that the earlier delete/remove-roles commands' exit codes were honest. Compare this directly to chapter 10's own framing: "the mover event should trigger a review of the identity's entire current access, not only the addition of the new role's access" — this is that review, and its result is a complete replacement of Priya's access, not a superset of it.

Jane's role mapping is untouched by any of this — a check worth running independently, because the whole point of a correctly scoped Mover event is that it changes exactly one identity's access, not a side effect on anyone else's:

docker compose exec keycloak /opt/keycloak/bin/kcadm.sh get \
  "users/404ef293-45d4-4716-8fc7-5c16592b6f20/role-mappings/realm" -r northwind-apps --fields name
[ {
  "name" : "tenant-admin"
}, {
  "name" : "default-roles-northwind-apps"
} ]

Case study: Segregation of Duties, designed into the role set itself

Case study — Meridian Trust's toxic combinations, and role design

Chapter 10 defined a Meridian Trust toxic combination: a teller who can both create a transfer and approve it has no independent check on their own actions. Nothing about Keycloak's roles/groups model prevents a toxic combination from being created — Keycloak enforces whatever roles you assign, it doesn't know what a toxic combination is (chapter 10 made the same point about access reviews generally). But role design is still a real lever: if Meridian defines transfer-creator and transfer-approver as two separate, narrow roles rather than folding both abilities into one broad teller role, then granting both to the same person requires someone to make two separate, visible assignment decisions instead of one — and an access review (chapter 10) can check "does any identity hold both roles" as a single, mechanical query against the role-assignment table, instead of having to reverse-engineer which permissions a monolithic role secretly bundles together. A well-designed role set doesn't enforce SoD by itself, but it makes a toxic combination visible and checkable instead of hidden inside one convenient role — which is exactly what Northwind's own three independent, non-composite roles in this chapter were designed to preserve.

Common mistakes

  • If you see a Mover event's checklist end after the "add" commands, the cause is treating chapter 10's warning as something that applies to other organizations' processes, not to the exact two kcadm.sh commands (or two Admin Console clicks) that were just run — the fix is always a remove-roles/group-removal step paired with every add-roles/ group-join step for a role change, not an addition on its own.
  • If you see someone confirm a role change only by checking that the new role now appears, the cause is checking half the state — the before/after comparison in this chapter deliberately re-ran the same query twice, so the old role's absence is evidence, not an assumption.
  • If you see a composite role used to bundle a "starter" role inside a "senior" role without documenting it, the cause is forgetting that composite roles are transitively granted — moving someone off the senior role doesn't help if the starter role was never independently revocable in the first place.
  • If you see every ability a job function needs folded into one broad role instead of several narrow ones, the cause is optimizing for fewer assignment clicks today at the cost of making a future toxic combination invisible to an access review later.
  • If you see a client role used where a realm role was needed (or vice versa), the cause is not asking "is this true of this person everywhere in the realm, or only inside one specific application" before picking which namespace the role belongs in.
  • If you see kcadm.sh's silent success on add-roles/update group calls treated as "nothing happened," the cause is not knowing that's simply this CLI's normal behavior — confirm with a separate get call, as every step in this chapter did, rather than re-running the same mutating command to "see if it works."

Takeaways

  • Realm roles are visible to every client in a realm; client roles are a namespace dedicated to one specific client — the scope difference decides which one fits a given decision.
  • A composite role grants every role "associated" with it from a single assignment; it wasn't used in this lab specifically so the Mover payoff's role removal stayed visible and unambiguous.
  • A group is a named collection of users that can carry its own role mappings and attributes, inherited automatically by every current member — add or remove a membership and the inherited access changes immediately.
  • This chapter created three independent realm roles (tenant-admin, support-agent, platform-engineer) and two groups (Engineering, Support) in a real northwind-apps realm, confirmed with real kcadm.sh output at every step.
  • Chapter 10's Mover event — moving Priya from support to platform engineering — was reproduced for real: grant the new role and group, then explicitly remove the old role and group, confirmed by re-running the same before-state query and seeing the old access gone.
  • Skipping the removal half of a Mover event is identity governance's most common beginner mistake precisely because the "add" half works, looks complete, and produces no error — nothing forces the removal step to happen on its own.
  • Every Keycloak realm automatically creates a default-roles-<realm> composite role that every user holds, carrying baseline roles like offline_access and uma_authorization — it showed up in every role-mapping query in this chapter's lab and is not something a Mover event needs to touch.
  • Keycloak's roles/groups model doesn't enforce Segregation of Duties on its own — it enforces whatever is assigned — but designing narrow, non-overlapping roles (rather than one broad role per job title) keeps a toxic combination visible to an access review instead of hidden inside a convenient bundle.
  • kcadm.sh's add-roles/remove-roles and the group-membership update/delete calls are exact mirror pairs — grant and revoke use the same underlying mechanism in opposite directions, which is part of why a Mover event is "two adds and two removes," not a fundamentally different operation.
