    47. Case Study: Riverside Health

    Riverside Health has been this course's running example for one specific hard problem since chapter 2: a single organization that has to run a strict, regulated workforce identity program and a patient-facing consumer identity program at the same time, under HIPAA, without letting either one compromise the other. This chapter doesn't teach a new concept — it's where every earlier chapter that touched Riverside gets pulled together into one coherent design, the way a real IAM specialist would actually be asked to present it: as an architecture, with trade-offs named and alternatives considered.

    Why this matters

    Nobody hires an IAM specialist to recite the OAuth spec. They hire one to take a messy brief — "secure the clinician portal and the patient portal, satisfy our HIPAA auditor, and don't make nurses hate their shift start" — and turn it into a realm design, a set of policies, and a written justification for each choice. This chapter is that exercise, worked end to end for Riverside, and the full write-up this chapter points to (case-studies/cs02-riverside.html) is written in the voice you'd use in an actual design doc or an interview answer.

    What concept is this?

    Nothing here is new. This chapter recalls: the identity lifecycle and the two-population split (chapter 2), directory federation (chapters 4, 7, 32), authentication factors and risk-based/step-up auth (chapter 5), ABAC/ReBAC authorization (chapters 6, 38), HIPAA's Security and Privacy Rules (chapter 11), realm design (chapter 21), required actions (chapter 22), and admin-event auditing (chapter 28) — all applied to one organization instead of treated as separate topics.

    The brief: two populations, one regulator

    Riverside Health is a regional hospital network (chapter 2). Its identity population splits into clinician workforce identities — doctors, nurses, and administrative staff who need fast, reliable access to clinical systems, often from shared workstations — and patient identities, who use a patient portal to view records, message their care team, and pay bills. Every one of those identities sits inside the HIPAA Security Rule's scope (chapter 11), which means strong authentication, tight audit trails, and access that maps to a legitimate clinical need aren't nice-to-haves; they're the actual deliverable.

    Two requirements drive almost every design decision in this chapter:

    • 45 CFR §164.312 (Technical Safeguards) — access control, audit controls, integrity, and transmission security, mapped directly onto authentication strength, authorization granularity, and event logging.
    • 45 CFR §164.502(b) (Minimum Necessary) — a clinician's access must be scoped to what their role and current relationship to a patient actually requires, not to "everything the hospital has," even though broad access would be operationally simpler to configure.
    Case study — Riverside Health

    Chapter 11 put it plainly: minimum necessary is a compliance requirement; a relationship-based access model that grants a clinician access only while an active care-team relationship exists is one concrete implementation of it — the one this chapter builds toward. Riverside is the epicenter of HIPAA across this course's three orgs precisely because its entire workforce and patient population sit inside this rule's scope.

    Design decision: two realms, not one

    Chapter 21 established that a realm is a fully isolated management namespace — its own users, its own authentication flows, its own session policy. Riverside's design uses two realms: riverside-clinicians for the workforce (clinicians and administrative staff) and riverside-patients for the patient portal population. They are not the same realm with a role split, and that's a deliberate trade-off, not a default.

    Alternative considered and rejected: one realm, two groups ("Clinicians" and "Patients"), each with its own role mappings. Rejected because a realm's authentication flow, password policy, and session timeouts are realm-wide settings, not group-scoped — a shared realm would force clinician-grade session timeouts onto patients (hostile to a portal meant for occasional, unhurried use) or patient-grade timeouts onto clinicians (unacceptable on a shared clinical workstation). Chapter 19's realm-isolation guarantee exists for exactly this situation: two populations with genuinely different security postures belong in different realms, not different groups within one.

    Clinician workforce: federate, don't migrate

    Chapter 4 modeled Riverside's hospital directory as an LDAP tree rooted at dc=riversidehealth,dc=org, with clinicians like cn=Dr. Amara Chen and cn=Dr. Liam Osei under ou=Clinicians and administrative staff like cn=Priya Nair under ou=Staff. Chapter 7 and chapter 32 turned that into a concrete decision: riverside-clinicians federates against this Active Directory rather than importing roughly ten thousand clinician accounts into Keycloak's own store.

    Case study — Riverside Health

    Chapter 4's self-check answer already made the business case directly: Riverside's AD already holds shift schedules, department assignments, and badge-linked accounts built up over years. Migrating all of that is slow, risky, and creates two copies of the truth that drift apart. Federating instead lets nurses keep the credentials they already have and lets HR keep managing joiners and leavers in the one place it already does — Keycloak authenticates against what's authoritative instead of replacing it.

    Edit mode decision: chapter 32 left this open as a self-check question — Riverside's federation provider stays in READ_ONLY mode, not WRITABLE. A clinician's AD account is owned by hospital IT and HR systems upstream of Keycloak; letting Keycloak write back (e.g. a self-service password reset flowing into AD) would make Keycloak a second place credential changes can originate from, undermining the "one authoritative source" property federation exists to protect. Password resets for clinicians go through the hospital's existing AD self-service tooling, not through Keycloak.

    Authentication strength: fast by default, stronger on demand

    Chapter 5 established Riverside's baseline for shared clinical workstations: a possession-factor badge tap, often paired with a short PIN — a deliberately weaker guarantee than a full second factor, chosen because authentication friction on a shared terminal costs clinical seconds. That trade-off is only acceptable because of what chapter 5's self-check named as its compensating controls: short session timeouts, workstation-level restrictions, and — critically for this chapter — audit logging that makes misuse detectable even though the authentication step alone doesn't prevent it.

    The badge-tap baseline is not Riverside's ceiling. A required action (chapter 22's mechanism) steps a clinician up to a full MFA challenge before specific high-sensitivity operations: opening the chart of a patient outside their current care team (rare, and logged regardless of outcome — see the pitfall below), or amending a finalized clinical note. The authentication strength scales with the sensitivity of what's being requested, not with a single realm-wide policy.

    Authorization: minimum necessary as an enforced rule, not a policy document

    Chapter 38 worked this out concretely for Riverside already: a clinician may view a patient record only if their department attribute matches the patient's assigned department, and only during their scheduled shift hours. Both conditions are attributes evaluated at request time, which puts this squarely inside Keycloak Authorization Services' native ABAC/PBAC ceiling (chapter 37) — two policies (a department-attribute policy, a shift-time policy) combined under a unanimous decision strategy, so the record only releases when both agree.

    Chapter 6 framed the ceiling above that: a true "currently on this patient's care team" relationship — which changes as a patient is transferred between departments or a clinician is added to a case — is a ReBAC edge, not an attribute. Keycloak's native authorization model has no relationship-tuple concept (chapter 38's finding), so Riverside's design uses the department+shift ABAC rule as the practical, natively-supported approximation of "currently responsible for this patient," accepting that it's an approximation (a clinician in the right department during the right shift who isn't actually on this specific patient's care team today would still pass) rather than reaching for an external ReBAC engine for a first version of this system.

    Pitfall

    If a Riverside clinician reports they can see a record they shouldn't be able to at 2 a.m., check the permission's decision strategy before the individual policies. Each policy can be written correctly in isolation and still produce the wrong combined result under an affirmative strategy instead of unanimous — a composition bug, not a policy-authoring bug, and invisible if you only re-read each policy's logic on its own (chapter 38).

    The exception path: break-glass, deliberately audited

    Minimum necessary cannot be absolute in a hospital: an attending physician covering an emergency may need to open a patient's record with zero advance notice, before any care-team relationship or department assignment has been set up. Riverside's design adds one deliberate escape hatch — break-glass access — rather than loosening the standing ABAC rule to cover emergencies, which would weaken minimum necessary for every ordinary request to accommodate a rare one.

    Mechanically: a clinician who fails the standing department+shift permission is offered a self-attested "emergency access" required action instead of a flat denial. Accepting it grants time-boxed access (typically one shift) and — unlike the ordinary access path — fires a dedicated, high-visibility admin event on every single use, flagged for same-week compliance review rather than sitting in the general event stream. The trade-off is explicit: Riverside accepts that break-glass can be used when it technically wasn't medically necessary, in exchange for never blocking a clinician from reaching a patient during a real emergency. The control that makes this acceptable is the audit, not the gate.

    Audit controls: 45 CFR §164.312(b), made concrete

    Chapter 28 already named the exact citation this chapter's audit mechanism answers: Audit controls (45 CFR §164.312(b)) — "hardware, software, and procedural mechanisms that record and examine ePHI system activity." With admin events and adminEventsDetailsEnabled=true switched on, that's not abstract: it's the timestamped CREATE/DELETE pair chapter 28's lab produced for a role-mapping change, attributable to a specific admin user id, that lets Riverside's compliance team answer "who had full chart access, when, and who approved it" for any shift-based elevation.

    Pitfall

    Chapter 28 flagged this exactly: adminEventsDetailsEnabled's full representation field can capture more of a changed record's contents than a HIPAA minimum-necessary review wants sitting in a database indefinitely. If you see Riverside's compliance team asking why the audit log itself contains more PHI than expected, the cause is this field, not a separate leak — review retention and field-level redaction on the event store, don't just disable the setting and lose the audit trail the Security Rule requires in the first place.

    Common mistakes

    If you see this, the cause is usually that
    • A clinician is logged out mid-procedure. The realm's session timeout was copied from a generic Keycloak example instead of being set deliberately short for a shared clinical workstation, or copied from the patient-portal realm by mistake — a strong argument for keeping the two realms separate rather than tuning one shared timeout for two incompatible use cases.
    • A patient can't view a clearly-theirs lab result. Email verification (chapter 22's required action) is still pending, or the patient's account was created by staff instead of self-registered and never triggered the verification flow at all.
    • The compliance team finds the audit log itself contains PHI it shouldn't retain this long. adminEventsDetailsEnabled's representation field, not a separate data leak — see the audit-controls slide above.
    • A clinician who changed departments last month can still see their old department's patients. The department attribute lives in AD (federated, read-only from Keycloak's side) and wasn't updated promptly upstream — the ABAC policy is working correctly; the source attribute is stale.
    • Break-glass access is being used routinely instead of rarely. This is a process signal, not a configuration bug: the standing ABAC rule's department or shift data is probably wrong or stale for a specific team, making the "exception" path the only one that works for them day to day. Fix the underlying attribute data, don't normalize the exception.

    Glossary

    Takeaways

    • Riverside's two realms exist because its clinician and patient populations need genuinely different authentication strength, session policy, and self-service — not because of an arbitrary preference for separation.
    • Federating the clinician workforce against the hospital's existing AD (read-only) keeps HR's joiner/mover/leaver process authoritative instead of creating a second, drifting copy of the truth.
    • Badge-tap MFA is an acceptable trade-off on shared clinical workstations only because it's paired with short sessions, workstation controls, and auditing — speed alone wouldn't justify it.
    • Step-up authentication scales authentication strength with the sensitivity of the specific action requested, not with a single realm-wide policy.
    • HIPAA's minimum-necessary standard is implemented here as a department-plus-shift ABAC rule under a unanimous decision strategy — a practical approximation of a true care-team relationship, which Keycloak's native model can't express directly.
    • Break-glass access is a deliberate, audited exception path, not a loophole — it trades a hard denial for mandatory, flagged logging, and should stay rare in practice.
    • Admin-event auditing with full details turns HIPAA's audit-controls requirement into a concrete, queryable record — but the same detail field can itself become a retention liability for PHI-adjacent changes.
    • Every one of these decisions is a trade-off with a named alternative that was considered and rejected for a stated reason — that written justification is what separates a design from a guess.
    • None of Riverside's mechanisms are HIPAA-specific Keycloak features; they're general IAM concepts (federation, ABAC, step-up auth, audit trails) pointed at a specific regulatory requirement.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first.

    1. Why does Riverside use two separate realms instead of one realm with a "Clinicians" and a "Patients" group?

      Model answerAuthentication flow, password policy, and session timeout are realm-wide settings in Keycloak, not settings you can scope to a group. A shared realm would force one of the two populations to live with the other's security posture — either clinician-grade short timeouts on a leisurely patient portal, or patient-grade long timeouts on a shared clinical workstation. Two realms let each population's settings reflect its own actual risk and usage pattern.

    2. Riverside's clinician federation provider is READ_ONLY, not WRITABLE. What would change, concretely, if it were switched to WRITABLE, and why did Riverside reject that?

      Model answerWRITABLE mode would let actions inside Keycloak — most plausibly a self-service password reset — write back into the hospital's Active Directory. That makes Keycloak a second place a clinician's credential can originate a change from, which undermines the entire point of federating against AD as the single authoritative source. Riverside keeps password resets inside the hospital's existing AD tooling and keeps Keycloak strictly a reader of that directory.

    3. Explain, without using the word "ReBAC," why the department+shift ABAC rule is only an approximation of "this clinician is currently on this patient's care team."

      Model answerThe ABAC rule checks two attributes true at request time — does the clinician's department match the patient's department, and is it currently their shift — neither of which actually records whether this specific clinician has been assigned to this specific patient's case. A clinician in the right department during the right shift who has never been assigned to this patient would still pass the rule. It's a reasonable stand-in given Keycloak's native authorization model has no way to represent a direct, assignable link between one clinician and one patient's case.

    4. A clinician's break-glass access was used three times last week by the same person for the same patient. Is this evidence of abuse, and what should an IAM specialist check first?

      Model answerNot automatically abuse — check first whether the standing ABAC rule's underlying attribute data (the clinician's department assignment, or the patient's assigned department) is stale or simply wrong for this case, which would make break-glass the only path that works even for entirely legitimate, repeated, routine access. Repeated break-glass use is a signal to fix the standing rule's data, not automatically a disciplinary matter — though it should still be reviewed, since the audit trail exists precisely so it can be.

    5. Why is enabling adminEventsDetailsEnabled both the right HIPAA-compliance move and a potential new compliance risk at the same time?

      Model answerIt directly implements the Security Rule's audit-controls requirement (45 CFR §164.312(b)) by producing a concrete, attributable record of who changed what and when — exactly what an auditor asks for. But its full representation field can capture more of a changed record's actual contents than a minimum-necessary review wants retained indefinitely in a separate event store, so the fix is retention/redaction policy on that store, not disabling the setting and losing the audit trail HIPAA requires in the first place.

    6. A colleague suggests simplifying Riverside's design by loosening the standing ABAC rule to also cover emergencies, instead of maintaining a separate break-glass path. What's wrong with that simplification?

      Model answerLoosening the standing rule to cover the rare emergency case would weaken minimum-necessary enforcement for every ordinary request, all the time, to accommodate a case that should stay rare and visible instead. Keeping break-glass as a separate, explicitly audited exception path preserves the strict rule for the common case while still allowing the rare case — at the cost of a flagged, reviewed audit event every time it's used, which is the correct trade: convenience in the rare case, paid for with visibility.
