    38. Fine-Grained Access Patterns: ABAC/ReBAC with Keycloak, and a Brief OPA/Cedar Comparison

    This chapter has no lab of its own — it's the synthesis chapter that closes a loop opened twice already. Chapter 6 surveyed ACL/RBAC/ABAC/PBAC/ReBAC/Zero Trust as vendor-agnostic models. Chapter 37 made one of them concrete: Keycloak's Authorization Services, a real, policy-based (PBAC-flavored, often ABAC-flavored) engine you configured and drove through a real UMA 2.0 flow. This chapter asks the honest question a working IAM specialist eventually has to answer out loud in a design review: what can Keycloak's own authorization model actually express, where does it genuinely run out, and what do you reach for next?

    Why this matters

    Every authorization model has a shape it's good at and a shape it isn't. An IAM specialist who only knows "Keycloak has policies, so just write a policy for it" will eventually try to force a relationship-shaped requirement — "a manager can approve a transaction for branches they manage" — into a tool that was never built to model relationships, and either ship something fragile or burn weeks discovering why it doesn't fit. Knowing the honest boundary ahead of time, and knowing what a purpose-built engine (OPA, Cedar, a Zanzibar-style ReBAC store) actually buys beyond that boundary, is the difference between a design review that ends in twenty minutes and one that ends in a production incident six months later.

    What concept is this?

    This is chapter 6's RBAC/ABAC/PBAC/ReBAC spectrum, revisited with chapter 37's real Keycloak mileage under your belt. Chapter 6 already previewed the punchline in its own "In Keycloak" callout on the ReBAC slide: "Keycloak doesn't ship a dedicated graph-based ReBAC engine, but its Authorization Services can express attribute- and relationship-flavored policies on top of its resource/scope/policy model." This chapter spells out exactly what "flavored" means in practice — where the approximation holds up, and where it quietly breaks — and then looks at two dedicated policy engines (OPA and Cedar) that exist precisely because plenty of real systems need more than "flavored."

    The spectrum, with Keycloak's coverage marked on it

    Chapter 6 ordered the five models roughly by how dynamic and relationship-aware the access decision is. Laid out that way, Keycloak's actual coverage — what you built with real primitives in chapters 23 and 37 — stops cleanly in the middle of that line, not at the end of it.

    • RBAC — realm and client roles, assigned to users or groups (chapter 23). Fully native, no approximation needed.
    • ABAC / PBAC — Authorization Services policies: role, user, group, time, client, client-scope, and aggregated policies, composed with a decision strategy (chapter 37). Fully native for anything expressible as "evaluate these attributes/conditions against this request right now." This is Keycloak's real ceiling — not a workaround, a first-class feature.
    • ReBAC — no first-class concept of a relationship tuple ("Alex manager_of Downtown Branch") anywhere in Keycloak's data model. What looks relationship-shaped has to be re-expressed as a group membership, a user attribute, or a custom policy that calls out to something that does store relationships — which is an approximation, not native support, and the rest of this chapter shows exactly where that approximation holds and where it doesn't.

    Worked ABAC example, fully native: Riverside Health shift-and-department access

    Requirement: Riverside Health needs a rule for its clinician portal: a clinician may view a patient record only if their department attribute matches the patient's assigned department, and only during their scheduled shift hours. Both conditions are attributes evaluated at request time — department is a user attribute, shift hours are a time condition — not a relationship and not a static role. This is squarely inside Keycloak's native ABAC/PBAC ceiling from the previous slide.

    In Authorization Services terms (chapter 37's vocabulary, used directly here): this is two separate policies — a group/attribute-based policy checking "clinician's department attribute equals the record's department claim" and a time-based policy checking "current time falls inside the clinician's configured shift window" — combined into one permission with the unanimous decision strategy, so the record is only released when both policies agree. Decision strategy choice here is not cosmetic: using the default affirmative strategy instead of unanimous would let a clinician view the record whenever either condition is true on its own — including outside shift hours, as long as the department matches — which is exactly the kind of subtle Authorization Services misconfiguration chapter 37's decision-strategy diagram warned about.

    Pitfall

    If a Riverside clinician reports they can see a record they shouldn't be able to at 2 a.m., the first thing to check is the permission's decision strategy, not the individual policies. Each policy in isolation might be written correctly and still produce the wrong combined result under an affirmative strategy — this is a composition bug, not a policy-authoring bug, and it's invisible if you only re-read each policy's logic on its own.

    Where it genuinely runs out: Meridian Trust's manager-of relationship

    Requirement: Meridian Trust needs: an employee may approve a transaction only if they have a manager-of relationship with the branch that owns the account. This is not an attribute of the employee (their department doesn't encode which specific branches they manage), not a static role (role alone says "this person is a manager," not "manager of this specific branch"), and it changes shape over time as managers rotate between branches. It is a genuine relationship edge — exactly chapter 6's ReBAC definition — between two specific entities, Alex and Downtown Branch, and a second edge between Downtown Branch and the account in question.

    You could try to flatten this into Keycloak's native primitives, and real teams do — but look honestly at what each approximation costs:

    • Group-per-relationship. Create a Keycloak group per branch, branch-managers-downtown, and put Alex in it. A role/group policy can now check "is this user in branch-managers-downtown." This works for a small, stable number of branches — but it means a group (and a policy referencing it) per branch, group membership changes are the only record of the relationship (no "since when," no easy audit of "who manages what" as a queryable graph), and a manager who covers twelve branches needs twelve group memberships kept in sync by hand or by a separate provisioning job.
    • Custom attribute encoding. Store a user attribute like manages_branches=downtown,eastside and write a custom policy (a Policy SPI implementation, chapter 39 territory) that parses it and checks membership. This scales better than one group per branch, but now the relationship data lives in a delimited string inside Keycloak's user attribute store — no native validation that referenced branches exist, no reverse query ("who manages this branch"), and every consumer of that relationship has to agree on the encoding.
    • Custom Policy SPI calling an external relationship store. Write a Keycloak Policy provider (chapter 39's SPI mechanism) whose logic is just "ask an external service — a real relationship store — whether this edge exists," and let Keycloak's policy evaluation defer to that answer. This is the honest middle ground: Keycloak still issues the final permit/deny and still sits in the request path, but the relationship graph itself lives somewhere actually built to store and query graphs.
    Pitfall

    The group-per-relationship and custom-attribute approximations both degrade the same way as the relationship gets more dynamic: more entities, more frequent changes, or multi-hop traversals (manager of a branch that owns an account, not just "manager of a branch" directly) each add friction that a real relationship-tuple store doesn't have. If you notice yourself writing a migration script to rename a group every time a manager moves branches, that's the signal the approximation has stopped paying for itself — not a sign to add a third workaround on top of the first two.

    What a purpose-built ReBAC engine actually adds

    Google's 2019 paper "Zanzibar: Google's Consistent, Global Authorization System" described the relationship-tuple model behind access control for Google Drive, Calendar, and Photos: authorization data stored as simple tuples — object#relation@subject, e.g. branch:downtown#manager@user:alex — plus a query language for checking and expanding relationships, including relationships defined in terms of other relationships (so "can approve transactions on account 1234" can be defined as "is manager of the branch that owns account 1234" and the engine walks that chain for you). OpenFGA (a Linux Foundation-hosted, CNCF Sandbox project, modeled directly on the Zanzibar paper) is the most widely used open-source implementation of this pattern today — this course did not independently re-verify OpenFGA's exact current CNCF maturity level or release version beyond confirming the project is active and Zanzibar-derived; treat the specific "Sandbox" designation as approximate and check openfga.dev directly before citing it precisely in anything graded on accuracy.

    The concrete thing this buys over Keycloak's approximations: the relationship graph is the system's actual data model, not a side-encoding bolted onto a user-attribute string. Multi-hop checks ("manager of the branch that owns this account") are first-class queries, not something a custom SPI has to re-implement by hand. Relationship changes (a manager rotating branches) are a single tuple write, immediately queryable, with no group-rename migration anywhere.

    Case study — Meridian Trust, continued

    Meridian Trust's realistic end state for the manager-of requirement, past a certain scale, is not "a cleverer Keycloak policy" — it's a relationship store (OpenFGA or an equivalent) holding the manager_of and owns tuples, queried by a custom Keycloak Policy provider (or by the resource server directly, alongside Keycloak) at approval time. Keycloak still authenticates Alex and still issues the token that proves who Alex is; it simply stops being the system of record for who manages which branch, because that was never really an authentication-system fact to begin with.

    Open Policy Agent (OPA) and Rego

    Open Policy Agent (OPA) is a general-purpose policy engine — not specifically a ReBAC engine, though it can express relationship-style logic — that evaluates policies written in its own declarative query language, Rego, against JSON input, and returns a decision. OPA is a CNCF Graduated project (accepted to CNCF in March 2018, promoted to Incubating in April 2019, and promoted to Graduated on January 29, 2021 — graduation required a completed security audit, defined governance, and the CNCF Code of Conduct). Live-checked for this chapter: the current OPA release is v1.22.3, a bugfix release — this number will already be stale by the time you read it, so treat the version itself as illustrative and check github.com/open-policy-agent/opa/releases for the real current one rather than trusting this printed digit.

    OPA's deployment model is deliberately decoupled from any one application: it typically runs as a sidecar next to a service, or embedded as a library, and the calling service sends it a JSON request (the input document) and gets back a JSON decision — there is no required central server in the simplest deployment, though Styra (OPA's originating company) and others offer managed control planes for distributing and monitoring policies across many OPA instances. Rego itself is a logic-programming-flavored language purpose-built for writing rules over nested JSON, which gives it real expressive power for arbitrary attribute logic but a real learning curve — it does not read like a general-purpose programming language, and that's by design, not an accident.

    AWS Cedar

    Cedar is a policy language and evaluation engine originally built by AWS, open-sourced in May 2023 under the Apache License 2.0 (specification and SDK both, on GitHub under the cedar-policy organization). Its headline design goals, per AWS's own announcement, are fast and provably-safe evaluation — the language was designed alongside a formal model so that properties like "this policy can never grant more than intended" can be checked, not just tested. AWS's own production use of it is Amazon Verified Permissions, a managed authorization service built on Cedar.

    Live-verification note, stated honestly because the sources genuinely disagree: Cedar's version numbering is not one clean number. The language specification version and the cedar-policy SDK/crate version are tracked separately and can both be referred to as "version 4," which is confusing on its own; AWS's Amazon Verified Permissions documentation describes an upgrade path from Cedar language version 2 to version 4 (with an intermediate 3), and the cedar-policy GitHub repository's own release history shows the engine/CLI crate itself well past that — v4.14.1 was the latest tagged release checked for this chapter. Don't repeat "Cedar is at version X" as a single fact without specifying which of the two numbering schemes you mean and checking it live — this course couldn't fully reconcile them from public sources and says so rather than picking one number and presenting it as settled.

    Pitfall

    Treating "Cedar" and "Amazon Verified Permissions" as the same thing is a common mix-up. Cedar is the open-source language and evaluation engine, usable standalone, in any application, with no AWS dependency. Amazon Verified Permissions is one managed product built on top of it. You can adopt Cedar the language without adopting that specific AWS service, the same way you can adopt Rego without adopting Styra's commercial control plane.

    Keycloak Authorization Services, OPA, and Cedar, compared honestly

    None of these three is strictly "better" — they solve overlapping but distinct problems, and the right choice depends on what's already true about your system. Kept brief and non-promotional, per this chapter's own brief: no sales pitch for any of the three.

    Take the Riverside Health department-and-shift rule from earlier in this chapter and sketch, in a few lines of pseudocode or real Rego/Cedar syntax (your choice, no need to run it), how you would express the same "department matches AND inside shift window" logic in whichever of OPA or Cedar you haven't used before. Notice how much of the logic — two conditions, combined with a logical AND — is identical to what Keycloak's unanimous decision strategy already expressed; the difference is almost entirely in where that logic lives and who deploys it, not in what it says.

    Where would you actually plug in an external policy engine?

    The realistic integration point is almost never "replace Keycloak." Keycloak still authenticates the user and still issues the token that proves who they are and what coarse-grained roles/groups they hold. An external engine (OPA, Cedar, or a ReBAC store like OpenFGA) sits at the resource server — the same role chapter 37 introduced — either instead of, or alongside, Keycloak's own Authorization Services, deciding the fine-grained part of the question.

    The practical wiring usually takes one of two shapes: (1) a custom Keycloak Policy SPI provider (chapter 39) that calls the external engine from inside an Authorization Services policy evaluation, so Keycloak still issues the final RPT and everything downstream keeps trusting Keycloak's decision the way chapter 37 taught — or (2) the resource server calling the external engine directly, after validating the Keycloak-issued token itself, with Keycloak's Authorization Services not involved in that particular decision at all. Option 1 keeps one consistent decision-issuing system; option 2 is simpler to build but means two separate places enforce policy, which is its own operational risk if they ever drift out of sync.

    Common mistakes and pitfalls

    • If you see a Keycloak policy getting re-written over and over to try to capture "who manages whom," the cause is usually a genuine relationship being forced into an attribute or group model — the recurring edits are the approximation leaking, not a sign you haven't found the right policy combination yet.
    • If you see a design doc proposing "let's just put OPA in front of everything and stop using Keycloak's policies entirely," the cause is usually skipping the comparison table above — Keycloak's native ABAC/PBAC ceiling already covers a large share of real requirements, and replacing working, in-the-box policy evaluation with an extra service to integrate and operate is a cost that needs a genuine ReBAC (or cross-system) reason, not just unfamiliarity with Authorization Services.
    • If you see someone asserting a precise, unhedged version number for OPA or Cedar in a design doc or slide, the cause is usually copying a number from memory or an old blog post rather than checking the project's own release page — both projects ship frequently, and Cedar in particular has two different "version 4" meanings in play at once.
    • If you see a custom Policy SPI provider that calls out to an external relationship store on every single request with no caching, the cause is usually treating that external call as free — it's a network hop added to every authorization decision, and it needs the same latency/availability thinking as any other external dependency in a hot path.

    Takeaways

    • Keycloak's native authorization coverage runs from RBAC (chapter 23) through ABAC/PBAC (chapter 37's Authorization Services) — that's the real ceiling, not a limitation to apologize for.
    • Keycloak has no first-class relationship-tuple concept; anything genuinely ReBAC-shaped has to be approximated with groups, attributes, or a custom Policy SPI calling an external store.
    • Group-per-relationship and custom-attribute-encoding approximations both degrade as the relationship gets more dynamic, more numerous, or multi-hop — watch for migration scripts as the warning sign that the approximation has stopped paying for itself.
    • A genuine ReBAC requirement (Meridian Trust's "manager of the branch that owns this account") is a multi-hop relationship edge, not an attribute or a role — that's the clean test for whether you actually need a relationship-tuple store.
    • Google's Zanzibar paper described the relationship-tuple model now implemented by open-source engines like OpenFGA; Keycloak's own Authorization Services never adopted this model directly.
    • OPA (Rego) is a CNCF Graduated, general-purpose policy engine typically run as a sidecar or embedded library, decoupled from any single application.
    • Cedar is Apache-2.0 open source, AWS-originated, designed alongside a formal model for provable policy properties, and powers Amazon Verified Permissions — but is not itself a dedicated ReBAC engine.
    • Live-verified version and status claims for fast-moving open-source projects go stale almost immediately — this chapter names the release it checked and says explicitly where the sources themselves didn't agree (Cedar's two "version 4" numbering schemes), rather than flattening that into one confident, possibly-wrong number.
    • The realistic place to plug in an external policy engine is the resource server (the enforcement point), either behind a custom Keycloak Policy SPI or alongside Keycloak's own Authorization Services — not as a wholesale replacement for Keycloak's authentication role.

    Self-check

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Try answering it yourself first.

    1. A colleague says "Keycloak supports ReBAC — chapter 6's own In Keycloak callout says Authorization Services can express relationship-flavored policies." Is this accurate? What word is doing the misleading work?

      Model answerIt's misleading in exactly the way "flavored" signals. Keycloak can approximate some relationship-shaped decisions using groups, attributes, or custom policies, but it has no first-class relationship-tuple data model — no native concept of "Alex manager_of Downtown Branch" that can be queried, traversed multi-hop, or audited as a graph. "Flavored" means "can be bent to resemble," not "natively models." The distinction matters the moment the relationship becomes dynamic or multi-hop, where the approximation's cost shows up fast.

    2. Explain, in plain terms, why Riverside Health's "department matches AND inside shift hours" rule is ABAC, not ReBAC, even though it depends on two different conditions.

      Model answerBoth conditions are attributes of the subject and the request evaluated independently at decision time — the clinician's department, the clinician's shift window, and the current time — not an edge between two specific entities in a relationship graph. There's no "Alex is related to this specific record" fact being checked; there's "does this user's current attribute values satisfy these conditions," which is exactly ABAC's shape, two conditions or not.

    3. Why does the decision strategy (affirmative vs. unanimous) matter just as much as the individual policies themselves in the Riverside example?

      Model answerBecause the decision strategy decides how multiple individually-correct policies combine into one outcome. Under an affirmative strategy, a clinician whose department matches but who is outside their shift window would still be granted access, because only one of the two policies needs to agree — even though both policies, read on their own, are written correctly. The bug lives in the composition, not in either policy, which is why it's easy to miss during a code/config review that only checks each policy in isolation.

    4. Give one concrete signal that a group-per-relationship or custom-attribute ReBAC approximation in Keycloak has "stopped paying for itself," per this chapter.

      Model answerNeeding to write a migration or sync script whenever the relationship changes — e.g. renaming or re-provisioning a branch-managers-downtown group every time a manager rotates to a different branch. If the approximation requires ongoing manual or scripted upkeep just to stay accurate, that's the sign the relationship has outgrown what a group or attribute can cleanly represent.

    5. A teammate wants to rip out Keycloak's Authorization Services entirely and replace it with OPA for every authorization decision in the system, including the straightforward role checks. What should you ask them first?

      Model answerAsk what specific decisions actually need OPA's extra expressiveness versus what's already well inside Keycloak's native RBAC/ABAC/PBAC ceiling. If most of the system's checks are stable role or attribute logic that Authorization Services already handles natively, wholesale replacement adds an extra service to deploy, secure, and keep in sync with Keycloak's identity data, for marginal benefit on the majority of decisions — the comparison should be made decision-by-decision, not as an all-or-nothing platform swap.

    6. Why does this chapter refuse to state a single, confident version number for Cedar, when it was willing to state one for OPA?

      Model answerBecause live-checking Cedar surfaced a genuine ambiguity the sources didn't agree on: the Cedar "language specification version" and the "cedar-policy SDK/crate version" are tracked separately but are both casually called "version 4," and AWS's own Verified Permissions documentation and the cedar-policy GitHub releases describe different numbers under that same label. OPA's release page gave one unambiguous latest tag. Per this course's no-invented-facts rule, the honest move when sources disagree is to say so explicitly rather than pick one number and present it as settled.
